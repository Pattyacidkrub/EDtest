Pulmonary Embolism

ED framing: PE is a probability-and-severity problem. First decide if the patient is unstable, then use pretest probability to avoid both missed PE and indiscriminate CT scanning.

1st moveAssess ABCs, perfusion, oxygenation, and shock phenotype.
Rule-outLow gestalt only: PERC, then D-dimer if PERC positive.
DefinitiveCTPA for most stable patients; V/Q or leg US when CT is not ideal.
Do not missMassive PE: hypotension, arrest, severe hypoxemia, RV failure.

Red Flag Snapshot

  • SBP below 90 mm Hg, shock index above 1, syncope, or cardiac arrest.
  • New hypoxemia, escalating work of breathing, or cyanosis with a clear chest exam.
  • RV strain on ECG, POCUS, CT, troponin, or BNP.
  • Pregnancy/postpartum state, active cancer, recent surgery/trauma, prior VTE, estrogen use.
  • Unilateral leg swelling plus dyspnea or pleuritic chest pain.

Epidemiology, Why It Matters, and Pathophysiology

Why PE is high-yield

PE is common enough to consider in ED dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, unexplained tachycardia, and shock, but uncommon enough that indiscriminate testing creates false positives and harm. Mortality concentrates in early missed massive PE and patients with poor cardiopulmonary reserve.

Pathophysiology

Most clinically important PE begins as lower-extremity or pelvic venous thrombosis. Emboli obstruct pulmonary arteries, increase pulmonary vascular resistance, strain the right ventricle, worsen V/Q mismatch, and can trigger hypoxemia, hypocapnia, troponin leak, syncope, or obstructive shock.

Virchow triad at bedside

Stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability map to surgery, trauma, immobilization, prior VTE, malignancy, pregnancy/postpartum state, estrogen therapy, thrombophilia, central lines, obesity, inflammatory disease, heart failure, and older age.

Key History, Focused Exam, Red Flags, and Differential

History that moves probability

  • Sudden or progressive dyspnea.
  • Pleuritic chest pain, cough, hemoptysis, or unexplained tachycardia.
  • Syncope or near-syncope, especially with exertion.
  • Unilateral leg pain or swelling.
  • Recent surgery, trauma, long travel, bed rest, cast, or hospitalization.
  • Prior DVT/PE, thrombophilia, active cancer, estrogen exposure, pregnancy, or postpartum state.
  • Bleeding risks before anticoagulation: intracranial disease, active bleeding, recent surgery, severe thrombocytopenia, liver disease.

Focused physical exam

  • Vitals: HR, RR, BP trend, pulse oximetry at rest and with ambulation if appropriate.
  • Perfusion: mental status, skin, JVP, shock index, signs of RV failure.
  • Lungs: often clear; wheeze, rales, or pleural rub do not exclude PE.
  • Heart: tachycardia, loud P2, new murmur, signs of alternate diagnoses.
  • Extremities: unilateral swelling, calf/thigh tenderness, venous distention, phlegmasia.

Red flags

Shock, arrest, severe hypoxemia, syncope, RV strain, high clot burden, or need for escalating oxygen.

Do not anchor

Normal chest x-ray, normal oxygen saturation, or anxiety-like symptoms do not independently exclude PE.

Major differential

ACS, aortic dissection, pneumothorax, pneumonia, asthma/COPD, CHF, pericarditis/tamponade, sepsis.

Low-risk clue

Normal vitals, no hypoxemia, no RV strain, low bleeding risk, reliable follow-up, and negative Hestia/sPESI.

Initial Stabilization and Diagnostic Decision Pathway

1

Unstable first

If arrest, persistent hypotension, or severe respiratory failure: resuscitate, search rapidly for reversible causes, and treat suspected massive PE when probability is high.

2

Bedside data

ECG, CXR, gas as needed, troponin/BNP, lactate, CBC/CMP/coags, pregnancy test, and type/screen if thrombolysis or procedure is possible.

3

Probability frame

Use gestalt or Wells/Geneva. PERC is only for patients already judged low probability. High probability usually goes to imaging and often empiric anticoagulation.

4

Rule out safely

Low probability plus PERC negative: stop PE testing. Low/intermediate probability with PERC positive: D-dimer; use age-adjusted cutoff when appropriate.

5

Image when needed

CTPA is standard in stable patients. Use V/Q when contrast is problematic or pregnancy pathway favors it. Compression US can diagnose VTE when chest imaging is difficult.

6

Start anticoagulation

When probability is high and bleeding risk acceptable, anticoagulation should not wait for delayed imaging. Choose UFH if reversal, procedures, or renal failure are likely.

7

Escalate therapy

Massive PE with shock, arrest, severe hypoxemia, or impending collapse: consider systemic thrombolysis, catheter therapy, or surgical embolectomy.

8

Disposition decision

Use sPESI/Hestia, vitals, oxygen need, RV strain, biomarkers, bleeding risk, comorbid disease, anticoagulant access, and follow-up reliability.

Risk Stratification

PERC

Only after low gestalt: age below 50, HR below 100, SpO2 at least 95%, no hemoptysis, no estrogen, no prior VTE, no unilateral leg swelling, no recent surgery/trauma requiring hospitalization.

Wells/Geneva

Convert vague suspicion into low, intermediate, or high probability. Wells emphasizes DVT signs, PE more likely than alternatives, tachycardia, prior VTE, surgery/immobilization, hemoptysis, and cancer.

Massive PE

Cardiac arrest, obstructive shock, sustained SBP below 90, or SBP drop over 40 mm Hg with PE physiology. Treat as a time-critical reperfusion problem.

Submassive PE

Normotensive PE with RV dysfunction and/or positive troponin/BNP. These patients can deteriorate and usually need monitored admission.

Low-risk PE

No shock, no hypoxemia, no RV strain, low bleeding risk, good renal/hepatic profile for anticoagulant choice, and reliable access/follow-up.

sPESI

Higher risk if age over 80, cancer, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, HR at least 110, SBP below 100, or O2 saturation below 90%.

Hestia

Asks whether any practical reason prevents outpatient care: instability, oxygen need, procedure need, bleeding risk, renal/liver failure, pain control, pregnancy, or social barriers.

Subsegmental PE

Individualized. Treat many patients with recurrence risk, symptoms, DVT, pregnancy, cancer, or poor reserve; select low-risk patients may be observed after expert review.

ED Treatment Algorithm

Stable suspected or confirmed PE

  • Give oxygen for hypoxemia; avoid unnecessary hyperoxia in stable patients.
  • Assess bleeding risk before anticoagulation, including pregnancy, renal function, platelet count, active bleeding, recent surgery, and intracranial history.
  • Start anticoagulation when PE is confirmed or when probability is high and imaging will be delayed.
  • Choose DOAC for selected low-risk adults without pregnancy, severe renal/hepatic disease, high bleeding risk, antiphospholipid syndrome concern, or major drug interactions.
  • Use LMWH for many admitted or outpatient patients, pregnancy, and some cancer-associated VTE pathways.
  • Use UFH when severe renal impairment, high bleeding risk, procedural need, or possible thrombolysis makes rapid offset useful.

Unstable suspected massive PE

  • High-flow oxygen; ventilate carefully because intubation can worsen RV preload/afterload.
  • Use cautious fluids only if underfilled; start norepinephrine early for shock.
  • Use bedside echo and leg US if CT is unsafe or delayed.
  • Activate PE response, ICU, interventional radiology/cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, and hematology as local resources allow.
  • Consider systemic alteplase if massive PE and no prohibitive contraindication; consider catheter-directed therapy or embolectomy when lysis risk is high or lysis fails.

Resuscitation pearl

The crashing PE patient dies from RV failure and obstructive shock. Avoid reflex large-volume fluids; support RV perfusion pressure, reduce hypoxemia/acidosis, and move quickly toward reperfusion when indicated.

Pregnancy and renal/hepatic cautions

Pregnancy generally favors LMWH and avoids warfarin and most DOAC use. Renal failure favors UFH. Significant hepatic disease complicates DOAC and warfarin use. Any anticoagulant choice must include bleeding risk, drug interactions, weight, renal function, procedure likelihood, and follow-up access.

Disposition: ICU, Admission, Observation, Discharge

ICU / resuscitation bay

Massive PE, arrest, persistent hypotension, vasopressor need, severe hypoxemia, respiratory failure, thrombolysis/catheter therapy/embolectomy/ECMO consideration, or marked RV dysfunction with deterioration.

Admit / monitored bed

Submassive PE, RV strain, positive biomarkers, oxygen requirement, syncope, high clot burden, severe pain, high bleeding risk, renal/hepatic disease, pregnancy, active cancer, recurrent VTE, or uncertain diagnosis.

Discharge candidate

Low-risk PE by sPESI/Hestia and clinician judgment, normal BP, no oxygen need, no severe comorbidity or RV strain, low bleeding risk, medication in hand, education, reliable follow-up.

Common Exam Traps and Tintinalli-Style Pearls

Common exam traps

  • Using PERC in a patient who is not low pretest probability.
  • Ordering D-dimer in high-probability PE and being falsely reassured.
  • Calling PE excluded because oxygen saturation, chest x-ray, or ECG is normal.
  • Missing PE presenting as syncope, new atrial arrhythmia, wheeze, abdominal pain, or unexplained tachycardia.
  • Starting a DOAC in pregnancy, severe renal/hepatic disease, antiphospholipid syndrome concern, or major drug interaction without checking suitability.
  • Giving large fluid boluses to RV failure without reassessing response.
  • Ignoring bleeding contraindications before thrombolysis in a patient who is not crashing.

High-yield pearls

  • PE testing starts with probability, not with CT.
  • D-dimer is a rule-out tool for the right patient, not a severity marker.
  • CTPA diagnoses anatomy; echo, biomarkers, oxygen need, and BP define danger.
  • A proximal DVT on compression US can solve the VTE question when chest imaging is unsafe or delayed.
  • Massive PE is defined by hemodynamics, not clot size alone.
  • UFH is the flexible ED anticoagulant when the patient may need lysis, a procedure, or rapid reversal.
  • Low-risk outpatient PE requires both medical low risk and practical reliability.

MCQ Review Prompts

1. A low-gestalt patient has HR 84, SpO2 99%, age 32, no estrogen, prior VTE, hemoptysis, unilateral leg swelling, or recent surgery/trauma. Next PE step?

A. CTPA B. D-dimer C. No PE testing D. Alteplase

Answer

C. PERC-negative low-risk patients need no further PE testing.

2. Which patient should not be evaluated with PERC?

A. Low gestalt risk B. Clinician already thinks PE is likely C. Young patient with no risk factors D. Normal vital signs

Answer

B. PERC is only for patients already judged low probability.

3. A 78-year-old low/intermediate-risk patient has D-dimer 620 ng/mL FEU and the assay supports age adjustment. Interpretation?

A. Positive B. Negative by age-adjusted cutoff C. Massive PE D. Thrombolysis required

Answer

B. Age-adjusted FEU cutoff is age x 10, or 780 ng/mL.

4. Normotensive PE with RV dilation and elevated troponin is classified as:

A. Low risk B. Submassive/intermediate risk C. Massive PE D. No PE

Answer

B. RV dysfunction or biomarker elevation without hypotension is intermediate-risk/submassive PE.

5. Suspected massive PE is too unstable for CT. Echo shows severe RV dilation and no tamponade. What does this support?

A. PE excluded B. Emergent reperfusion consideration C. Outpatient V/Q D. D-dimer first

Answer

B. In shock, compatible RV strain can support immediate PE-directed therapy when CT is unsafe.

6. Best anticoagulant when severe renal failure and possible procedure/thrombolysis are concerns?

A. UFH B. Fondaparinux C. Rivaroxaban D. Warfarin alone

Answer

A. UFH is short acting, titratable, and reversible.

7. Most appropriate outpatient PE candidate?

A. Hypotensive patient B. Patient needing oxygen C. Low-risk Hestia/sPESI-negative patient with medication access and follow-up D. Positive troponin with RV strain

Answer

C. Outpatient PE care requires low medical risk plus reliable logistics.

8. Proximal noncompressible femoral vein on US in a dyspneic patient with contrast contraindication means:

A. VTE supported and treatment can proceed B. PE excluded C. Cellulitis only D. Anticoagulation contraindicated

Answer

A. Proximal DVT can establish VTE when chest imaging is difficult.

9. Major contraindication to systemic thrombolysis in a non-arresting PE patient?

A. Remote ankle sprain B. Active major bleeding or prior intracranial hemorrhage C. Mild pleuritic pain D. Sinus tachycardia

Answer

B. Active major bleeding and prior intracranial hemorrhage are major thrombolysis hazards.

10. Best one-line approach to suspected stable PE?

A. CT everyone B. Estimate probability, use PERC/D-dimer appropriately, image when indicated, anticoagulate confirmed/high-probability patients C. D-dimer everyone D. Alteplase all chest pain

Answer

B. This is the ED decision sequence.

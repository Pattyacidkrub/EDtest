Pulmonary Embolism

ED framing: PE is a probability and severity problem. First decide whether the patient is unstable; then use pretest probability to avoid both missed PE and unnecessary CT/anticoagulation.

First moveABCs, perfusion, oxygenation, shock index, contraindications to anticoagulation.
Rule-out laneLow gestalt only: PERC. If not PERC negative, use D-dimer when probability allows.
Definitive testCTPA for most stable adults; V/Q or leg ultrasound when CT is not ideal.
Do not missHigh-risk PE: hypotension, arrest, severe hypoxemia, RV failure, syncope.

Red Flag Snapshot

  • SBP <90 mm Hg, shock index >1, syncope, altered mental status, cardiac arrest.
  • Escalating oxygen need, cyanosis, respiratory muscle fatigue, or severe unexplained tachypnea.
  • ECG/POCUS/CT/biomarker evidence of right ventricular strain.
  • Pregnancy/postpartum, active cancer, recent surgery/trauma, prior VTE, estrogen exposure.
  • Unilateral leg swelling with dyspnea, pleuritic pain, hemoptysis, or tachycardia.

Epidemiology, Why It Matters, Pathophysiology

Why it matters

PE is common enough to consider in ED dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, unexplained tachycardia, and shock, but uncommon enough that indiscriminate CT creates false positives, contrast harm, and anticoagulation bleeding.

Pathophysiology

Most clinically important PE begins as lower-extremity or pelvic venous thrombosis. Emboli raise pulmonary vascular resistance, strain the RV, impair LV preload, worsen V/Q matching, and can cause obstructive shock.

Risk map

Tintinalli Table 56-1 groups risk by stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability: surgery, trauma, immobility, cancer, pregnancy, estrogen, thrombophilia, lines, obesity, inflammatory disease, heart failure, older age, and prior VTE.

Key History, Focused Exam, Differential

History that changes probability

  • Sudden or progressive dyspnea; pleuritic chest pain; cough; hemoptysis.
  • Syncope or near-syncope, especially with exertion or hypotension.
  • Unilateral leg pain/swelling, prior DVT/PE, recent surgery/trauma, cast, long travel, bed rest.
  • Active cancer, thrombophilia, estrogen therapy, pregnancy/postpartum, central venous catheter.
  • Bleeding risk before anticoagulation: intracranial disease, active bleeding, recent surgery, thrombocytopenia, severe liver disease.

Focused physical exam

  • Vitals: HR, RR, BP trend, pulse ox at rest and with exertion if safe.
  • Perfusion: mental status, cool skin, JVP, shock index, RV failure signs.
  • Lung exam may be clear; wheeze, rales, fever, or pleural rub do not exclude PE.
  • Heart: tachycardia, loud P2, new murmur, signs of ACS, tamponade, heart failure, or dissection.
  • Extremities: unilateral swelling, tenderness, venous distension, cyanosis, or phlegmasia.

Immediate threats

Massive PE, tension pneumothorax, ACS, aortic dissection, tamponade, severe asthma/COPD, sepsis.

Common mimics

Pneumonia, pleurisy, pericarditis, CHF, anxiety/hyperventilation, musculoskeletal chest pain.

Pediatric caveat

Wells/PERC are not as validated in children. Pediatric PE often relates to central lines, cancer, congenital heart disease, thrombophilia, infection, trauma, or estrogen.

Pregnancy caveat

Symptoms overlap normal pregnancy; use pregnancy-aware imaging and anticoagulation. LMWH is typical; warfarin/DOACs are generally avoided unless specialist-directed.

Initial Stabilization and Diagnostic Decision Pathway

1

Unstable first

Airway/oxygen, IV/IO access, monitor, pressors, bedside echo, ECG, CXR. If high suspicion and shock/arrest, treat as high-risk PE while excluding other killers.

2

Frame probability

Use gestalt plus Wells/Geneva. PERC is only for low pretest probability. High probability should go directly to imaging and often empiric anticoagulation.

3

Low-risk rule-out

Low probability + all PERC items negative = stop PE testing. If any PERC item positive, use D-dimer rather than immediate CT when appropriate.

4

D-dimer lane

Use in low/intermediate probability. Age-adjusted FEU cutoff for age >50 is age x 10 ng/mL if the assay/pathway supports it.

5

Image when needed

CTPA for most stable adults. V/Q if contrast/pregnancy strategy favors it. Compression US can establish VTE when chest imaging is delayed or unsafe.

6

Anticoagulate

If probability is high or VTE confirmed and bleeding risk acceptable, start anticoagulation. Choose UFH when rapid reversal/procedure/renal failure matters.

7

Reperfuse

High-risk PE with shock, arrest, severe hypoxemia, or collapse: systemic thrombolysis, catheter therapy, surgical embolectomy, or ECMO pathway depending on context.

8

Disposition

Use sPESI/Hestia, vitals, oxygen need, RV strain, biomarkers, bleeding risk, comorbidity, medication access, and follow-up reliability.

Risk Stratification

High-risk / massive

Arrest, sustained SBP <90, obstructive shock, severe hypoxemia, impending collapse. Reperfusion decision is time-critical.

Intermediate-high

Normotensive but RV dysfunction plus positive troponin/BNP or clinical deterioration. Admit monitored; rescue lysis/catheter if decompensating.

Intermediate-low

Normotensive with either RV strain or biomarker elevation, not both. Usually admission or observation depending on comorbidity and trajectory.

Low-risk

No shock, no oxygen need, no RV strain/biomarker concern, low sPESI/Hestia, low bleeding risk, reliable medication access and follow-up.

sPESI high-risk items

Age >80, cancer, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, HR >=110, SBP <100, oxygen saturation <90%. Any item makes sPESI positive and argues against routine outpatient care.

Hestia-style exclusions

Hemodynamic instability, need for thrombolysis/procedure, active bleeding/high bleeding risk, oxygen need >24 hr, PE while anticoagulated, severe pain needing IV opioids, medical/social reason for admission, CrCl <30, severe liver disease, pregnancy, or HIT concern.

ED Treatment Algorithm

High-risk PE

Oxygen, norepinephrine, cautious small fluid only if preload-responsive, UFH if not contraindicated, immediate reperfusion discussion. Systemic alteplase if shock/arrest and no prohibitive contraindication; catheter/surgery/ECMO when available or lysis unsafe.

Intermediate PE

Anticoagulate, monitor, assess RV and biomarkers, avoid routine lysis. Escalate if worsening BP, oxygenation, lactate, syncope, or RV failure.

Low-risk PE

Oral DOAC or LMWH pathway if eligible, education, bleeding precautions, medication access, and rapid follow-up. Do not discharge if oxygen need, RV strain, major comorbidity, bleeding risk, pregnancy, or unreliable follow-up.

Thrombolysis contraindication scan: prior intracranial hemorrhage, ischemic stroke or major head trauma, intracranial tumor/vascular lesion, active bleeding, recent major surgery/procedure, severe uncontrolled hypertension, thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy, or pregnancy/postpartum bleeding risk. In arrest or profound shock, risk-benefit may still favor lysis.

Disposition Criteria

ICU / resuscitation bay

Shock, arrest, thrombolysis/catheter therapy, escalating oxygen/ventilation, severe RV failure, persistent lactate, active bleeding on anticoagulation.

Admit / monitored bed

Intermediate-risk PE, positive troponin/BNP with RV strain, oxygen requirement, pregnancy, cancer with complexity, renal failure, high bleeding risk, poor reserve, uncertain diagnosis.

Possible discharge

Low-risk PE by sPESI/Hestia, normal perfusion/oxygenation, no RV strain, low bleeding risk, no severe comorbidity, drug access, education, reliable follow-up within days.

Exam Traps and Tintinalli-style Pearls

Common exam traps

  • PERC is invalid unless the patient is already low probability.
  • D-dimer cannot safely rule out high-probability PE.
  • Normal oxygen saturation, normal CXR, or absent S1Q3T3 does not exclude PE.
  • Syncope plus tachycardia or hypotension is a severity clue, not reassurance.
  • Subsegmental PE management is individualized; first exclude proximal DVT and recurrence risk.
  • Pregnancy and pediatric PE require tailored pathways; adult rules do not transfer cleanly.

High-yield pearls

  • Start with physiology: unstable PE is a reperfusion problem; stable PE is a probability problem.
  • Use leg ultrasound strategically when chest imaging is delayed, unsafe, or nondiagnostic.
  • RV strain plus biomarkers predicts decompensation even when BP is normal.
  • UFH is the ED anticoagulant when the patient may need thrombolysis, procedure, or rapid reversal.
  • Outpatient PE requires low-risk disease plus a low-risk patient plus a workable medication/follow-up plan.

MCQ Review Prompts

Q1. A 32-year-old with pleuritic chest pain is low gestalt probability, HR 86, SaO2 99%, no estrogen, no hemoptysis, no prior VTE, no unilateral leg swelling, no recent surgery/trauma. Best next step?
  1. Stop PE testing using PERC
  2. Send D-dimer anyway
  3. CTPA now
  4. Start alteplase

Answer: A. PERC is only for low pretest probability patients. If all criteria are negative, PE testing can stop.

Q2. Which patient should NOT be managed by PERC alone?
  1. Low-risk patient with all PERC items negative
  2. Patient with syncope, SBP 86, and RV dilation
  3. Young patient with reproducible chest wall pain and normal vitals
  4. Low-risk patient with no VTE risk factors

Answer: B. PERC is not for unstable or non-low-probability patients.

Q3. A 76-year-old low/intermediate probability patient has a high-sensitivity FEU D-dimer of 690 ng/mL. If the assay supports age adjustment, interpretation is:
  1. Positive because any value over 500 is positive
  2. Cannot use D-dimer in older adults
  3. Negative using age x 10 cutoff
  4. Diagnostic of PE

Answer: C. For patients older than 50, age-adjusted FEU cutoff is often age x 10 ng/mL.

Q4. Which finding most strongly pushes PE into high-risk/massive physiology?
  1. Normal chest x-ray
  2. Pleuritic pain only
  3. Isolated positive D-dimer
  4. Persistent hypotension or obstructive shock

Answer: D. Shock or persistent hypotension defines high-risk PE and changes treatment urgency.

Q5. Best anticoagulant choice when PE is likely, renal failure is severe, and catheter therapy may be needed:
  1. Unfractionated heparin
  2. Fondaparinux
  3. Rivaroxaban first dose
  4. Warfarin alone

Answer: A. UFH is short acting, titratable, and reversible.

Q6. A stable patient has positive proximal leg compression ultrasound while CTPA is delayed by contrast allergy workup. What does this mean?
  1. PE is impossible
  2. No treatment is needed
  3. Treat as VTE when clinical context fits
  4. Only thrombolysis is indicated

Answer: C. Proximal DVT establishes VTE and can justify treatment when chest imaging is unsafe or delayed.

Q7. Which feature argues against immediate discharge after PE diagnosis?
  1. Normal blood pressure and no oxygen need
  2. Troponin elevation with RV dilation
  3. Reliable follow-up
  4. Low bleeding risk

Answer: B. RV strain and positive biomarkers indicate higher risk and usually need monitored care.

Q8. Most important exam trap:
  1. S1Q3T3 is neither sensitive nor required
  2. A normal CXR does not rule out PE
  3. D-dimer is inappropriate as a rule-out test in high probability PE
  4. All are true

Answer: D. PE diagnosis is probability-based; no single normal basic test safely excludes it in the wrong pretest context.

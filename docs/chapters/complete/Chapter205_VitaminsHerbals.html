Toxicology Chapter 205

Vitamins and Herbals

Chapter 205 is an exposure-history chapter: the dangerous patient is often not the person who swallowed one tablet, but the patient with chronic supplement use, polypharmacy, pregnancy, anticoagulation, transplant/HIV therapy, or an unknown imported product.

Board lens: natural does not mean safe. Treat the physiology first, then hunt for the product identity, contaminant, adulterant, and interaction.
Pattern Fat-soluble vitamins create chronic toxicity; selected water-soluble vitamins still matter.
Trap Herbal labels may not predict dose, purity, contaminant, or herb-drug interaction.
Disposition Organ injury, severe toxidrome, pregnancy, anticoagulant/transplant/HIV interaction, or unknown product earns observation/admission.

Vitamins

Hypervitaminosis from fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K is usually subacute or chronic because stores accumulate. Tintinalli also flags the water-soluble exceptions: niacin, pyridoxine, and ascorbate.

How to read this section: ask what organ system is declaring itself. Vitamin A looks neurologic/dermatologic/hepatic; vitamin D is a hypercalcemia problem; vitamin E/K affect coagulation; B6 is neuropathy; vitamin C can become renal/assay trouble.

Vitamin-Specific Traps

Vitamin A

Excess preformed vitamin A may cause red peeling rash, headache, vomiting, blurred vision, abnormal pigmentation, hair loss, dry skin, pruritus, bone pain/fractures, liver failure, and pseudotumor cerebri. Treatment is discontinuation plus supportive care; beta-carotene does not behave like preformed vitamin A toxicity.

Vitamin D

Vitamin D toxicity is clinically a hypercalcemia syndrome: anorexia, nausea, abdominal pain, lethargy, weight loss, polyuria, constipation, confusion, and coma. ED care is to stop vitamin D/calcium exposure, give IV hydration, and treat significant hypercalcemia with calcitonin and/or bisphosphonate when needed.

Board trap: chronic supplement use can be the hidden cause of hypercalcemia. Do not anchor on malignancy or primary hyperparathyroidism before asking about OTC preparations.

Vitamin E, K, Folate

Vitamin E can worsen warfarin-associated coagulopathy and may inhibit platelet aggregation. Vitamin K toxicity is rare in routine forms, but menadione is toxic and banned for human use. Folate can normalize macrocytosis while B12 neurologic injury continues.

B Vitamins and Vitamin C

Chronic high-dose B6 causes sensory neuropathy with gait instability and loss of position/vibration sense; it can also inactivate levodopa when taken together. B12 toxicity is rare, but hydroxocobalamin can interfere with colorimetric lab tests. Large vitamin C doses may cause diarrhea, oxalate nephropathy/renal failure, and false-negative guaiac testing.

Herbal or Botanical Dietary Agents

Herbal preparations are common, culturally embedded, and often treated by patients as safer than pharmaceuticals. Tintinalli and Rosen both push the same ED stance: identity, dose, concentration, purity, and interactions may be uncertain.

Rosen source check

Rosen Ch.153 - regulation and product uncertainty

Rosen adds the practical explanation for why ED history is unreliable: herbal products are treated as foods rather than pharmaceuticals, many patients do not disclose them, and adverse events can come from inherent toxicity, contamination/adulteration, or herb-drug interaction.

Rosen vs Tintinalli

Tintinalli gives the chapter table structure; Rosen adds the bedside reason to distrust label purity and ask explicitly about nonprescription products.

Decision change

Do not stop at the named herb. Ask for bottle/photo, source country, dose schedule, coingestants, and prescription meds.

Some herbal agents are usually safe but still create allergic reactions, GI upset, anticoagulant interactions, sedation, or serotonergic interactions. These are not benign if the patient is anticoagulated, pregnant, immunosuppressed, or taking serotonergic/CYP-dependent drugs.

Herb-Drug Interactions

The exam-heavy interactions are not exotic: ginkgo plus aspirin/warfarin bleeds, St. John's wort lowers cyclosporine/digoxin/indinavir, grapefruit raises many CYP3A4 substrate levels, ephedra stacks with MAOI/sympathomimetics, and chronic licorice can create pseudohyperaldosteronism.

Rosen source check

Rosen Ch.153 - CYP/P-gp, serotonin, bleeding, and GABA sedation

Rosen expands the mechanism: interactions often run through CYP450 and p-glycoprotein, St. John's wort can contribute to serotonin syndrome with serotonergic drugs, and kava/valerian can potentiate GABA-mediated CNS depression with alcohol or sedatives.

Rosen vs Tintinalli

Tintinalli table names the interaction pairs; Rosen explains why interaction screening must include antidepressants, sedatives, anticoagulants, transplant drugs, and HIV therapy.

Decision change

Medication reconciliation is part of toxicology, not paperwork. Ask directly about warfarin/DOACs, SSRIs/SNRIs/MAOIs, benzodiazepines, alcohol, cyclosporine, digoxin, and antiretrovirals.

ED Management and Disposition

Management is usually supportive and syndrome-directed. The ED task is to identify the supplement, stop exposure, treat the physiologic consequence, and decide who is safe for discharge after observation.

History Product name/photo, dose, timing, duration, source country, batch, coingestants, prescription meds, pregnancy, anticoagulants, transplant/HIV drugs.
Testing Glucose, electrolytes, renal/hepatic function, CBC/coagulation profile, ECG when indicated, acetaminophen/salicylate if overdose unclear.
Contaminants Consider lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, selenium, cyanide, and product misidentification.
Rosen source check

Rosen Ch.153 - diagnostics and disposition

Rosen specifically broadens the workup for herbal toxicity to CBC, coagulation profile, electrolytes, glucose, hepatic and renal function, acetaminophen/salicylate when appropriate, and heavy metal testing when the syndrome suggests contamination.

Rosen vs Tintinalli

Tintinalli emphasizes supplement classifications and interaction tables; Rosen provides the ED diagnostic/disposition checklist.

Decision change

Mild GI symptoms with normal testing may be observed and discharged; systemic toxicity, organ injury, pregnancy risk, or unreliable product identity needs monitoring/consultation.

Disposition rule: admit or observe when there is organ injury, severe neuro/cardiovascular findings, abnormal coagulation, hypercalcemia/renal failure, pregnancy, intentional overdose, unknown/adulterated product, or high-risk interaction.

Inline MCQs

1. A patient taking chronic high-dose vitamin A has headache, blurred vision, dry skin, and hypercalcemia. Best ED framing?
Rationale: Tintinalli Ch205: hypervitaminosis A is usually chronic/subacute and may cause pseudotumor cerebri, hypercalcemia, and liver injury; treatment is discontinuation and supportive care.
2. Which vitamin toxicity most directly creates a hypercalcemia resuscitation problem?
Rationale: Vitamin D toxicity produces hypercalcemia with anorexia, abdominal pain, lethargy, polyuria, constipation, confusion, and coma.
3. A warfarin patient starts high-dose vitamin E and presents with mucosal bleeding. What is the key concern?
Rationale: Tintinalli lists chronic vitamin E toxicity as coagulopathy in warfarin patients plus nausea, fatigue, headache, weakness, and blurred vision.
4. Large folate doses may be dangerous because they can:
Rationale: Folate can mask macrocytic anemia from B12 deficiency while irreversible neurologic injury continues.
5. A patient taking pyridoxine chronically develops unstable gait and loss of vibration sense. Most likely toxicity?
Rationale: Chronic high-dose pyridoxine causes peripheral sensory neuropathy with gait instability and loss of position/vibration sense.
6. Which supplement history is most important in a transplant patient with low cyclosporine level?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 205-4 lists St. John's wort decreasing cyclosporine, digoxin, and indinavir levels; Rosen highlights CYP/P-gp interaction risk.

Assessment

7. A patient taking ginkgo plus aspirin has epistaxis and bruising. Most likely interaction?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 205-4 lists ginkgo with aspirin/warfarin as increased bleeding risk.
8. Which herbal product is most linked to pseudohyperaldosteronism with hypokalemia and reduced antihypertensive effect?
Rationale: Licorice can inhibit 11-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, causing pseudohyperaldosteronism, potassium loss, and reduced antihypertensive effect.
9. A patient using kava with alcohol has excessive sedation. Rosen adds which bedside warning?
Rationale: Rosen Ch153 emphasizes kava and valerian potentiating GABA-mediated CNS depression from alcohol or sedatives.
10. Which statement about herbal product regulation is most board-relevant?
Rationale: Tintinalli and Rosen both emphasize DSHEA/regulatory limits: product identity, concentration, contaminants, and interactions are uncertain at the bedside.
11. A herbal toxicity patient has abdominal pain, anemia, and neuropathy. Which diagnostic expansion is most appropriate?
Rationale: Rosen notes contaminants/adulterants, especially heavy metals, may cause toxicity from herbal medications.
12. Which herbal agent in Tintinalli Table 205-3 is associated with anticholinergic syndrome?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 205-3 lists lobelia as an expectorant/asthma treatment with anticholinergic syndrome.
13. A patient using grapefruit juice develops excess effects from several CYP3A4-metabolized drugs. What does Table 205-4 emphasize?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 205-4 lists grapefruit juice increasing availability of multiple drugs via intestinal CYP3A4 inhibition.
14. Which vitamin can cause false-negative guaiac testing for fecal occult blood at high doses?
Rationale: Tintinalli Ch205 notes large vitamin C doses may cause false-negative guaiac testing.
15. Which herbal agent was banned by FDA in 2004 after serious cardiovascular adverse effects?
Rationale: Rosen Ch153 notes FDA banned Ma Huang (Ephedra sinica) after reports of serious cardiovascular events.
16. A patient using pennyroyal oil has hepatitis. How should this be framed?
Rationale: Rosen and Tintinalli list pennyroyal among herbal products associated with hepatotoxicity.
17. A patient on MAOI takes ephedra. What is the board concern?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 205-4 lists ephedra with MAO inhibitors as increased toxicity and with sympathomimetics as additive effect.
18. In suspected herbal medication toxicity, Rosen suggests routine labs should include:
Rationale: Rosen Ch153 diagnostic testing recommends broad screening based on syndrome, including CBC, coagulation, electrolytes, glucose, hepatic/renal tests, and common coingestant levels.
19. A patient using herbal products during pregnancy asks if natural means safe. Best answer?
Rationale: Rosen warns against routine herbal medication use during pregnancy because toxicities and fetal effects are uncertain.
20. Which item in Table 205-2 may inhibit platelet aggregation and interact with warfarin?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 205-2 lists ginkgo as possibly inhibiting platelet aggregation and interacting with warfarin.
21. A patient with severe vitamin D toxicity and confusion is dehydrated and hypercalcemic. Initial ED treatment emphasis?
Rationale: Tintinalli Ch205: treatment includes stopping vitamin D, reducing calcium intake, IV hydration, calcitonin, and bisphosphonates for significant hypercalcemia.
22. Which vitamin form is specifically noted as toxic/banned for human use?
Rationale: Tintinalli notes menadione, a vitamin K form, is toxic and banned for human use.
23. A patient with herbal toxicity has mild GI symptoms and normal diagnostic testing. Disposition?
Rationale: Rosen disposition: many mildly symptomatic patients with normal testing can be observed, hydrated, and discharged; systemic toxicity or organ injury requires admission.
24. Which supplement in Tintinalli Table 205-2 may interact with serotonin reuptake inhibitors and should avoid tyramine-containing foods?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 205-2 lists St. John's wort with serotonin reuptake inhibitor interaction and tyramine warning.
25. A patient taking large vitamin B12 doses has abnormal colorimetric lab results. Which caveat matters?
Rationale: Tintinalli notes hydroxocobalamin can interfere with colorimetric lab tests, including tests used in poisoning evaluations.
26. Which approach best handles an unknown herbal product ingestion?
Rationale: Tintinalli/Rosen emphasize uncertain identity/concentration, contaminants, adulterants, interactions, and syndrome-directed ED care.

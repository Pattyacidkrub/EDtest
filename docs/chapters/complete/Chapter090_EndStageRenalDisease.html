Renal and Genitourinary Disorders

End-Stage Renal Disease

ESRD patients bring ordinary ED diagnoses plus dialysis physiology: uremia, fluid overload, hyperkalemia, access infection or bleeding, pericardial disease, immune dysfunction, and peritoneal dialysis complications.

Board trap: Do not let “dialysis patient” become the diagnosis; check access, schedule/missed dialysis, dry weight, urine output, uremic symptoms, potassium, volume, and infection source.

END-STAGE RENAL DISEASE

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

ESRD is irreversible loss of renal function requiring renal replacement therapy by transplant, hemodialysis, or peritoneal dialysis. Mortality is high; cardiac disease causes about half of deaths and infection accounts for many others.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Renal failure causes excretory failure with uremic toxins, biosynthetic failure from loss of erythropoietin and active vitamin D, and regulatory failure with oxidative and endocrine disturbances. Uremia is a clinical syndrome rather than a single lab value.

CLINICAL FEATURES OF UREMIA

Uremia may cause muscle cramps, pruritus, encephalopathy, dialysis dementia, neuropathy, CAD, hypertension, heart failure, pericarditis/tamponade, anemia, bleeding diathesis, immune dysfunction, GI symptoms/bleeding, ascites, and renal bone disease. BUN and creatinine do not reliably measure the severity of the clinical uremic syndrome.

NEUROLOGIC COMPLICATIONS

Stroke and subdural hematoma are more common in dialysis patients. Uremic encephalopathy can mimic stroke and is diagnosed after other structural, vascular, infectious, toxic, and metabolic causes are excluded. Focal deficits require neuroimaging.

CARDIOVASCULAR COMPLICATIONS

CARDIAC MARKERS

Troponin I/T can be chronically elevated from LVH and microvascular disease, yet still carries prognostic meaning. Suspect MI with symptoms, ECG changes, and dynamic troponin rise, often using a 20% or greater change with at least one value above the 99th percentile.

HYPERTENSION

ESRD hypertension begins with volume control, then adrenergic blockers, ACE inhibitors, and vasodilators such as hydralazine or minoxidil when needed.

HEART FAILURE

Heart failure is usually from hypertension, CAD, valvular disease, uremic cardiomyopathy, fluid overload, or high-output AV access. BNP is often elevated and lacks a clean volume-overload threshold.

UREMIC CARDIOMYOPATHY

Uremic cardiomyopathy is a diagnosis of exclusion after ischemia, hypertension, and hypoalbuminemia are considered. Dialysis rarely normalizes established LV dysfunction.

PULMONARY EDEMA

Use oxygen, noninvasive ventilation, nitrates, ACE inhibitor when appropriate, and dialysis for definitive volume removal. Loop diuretics may provide brief venodilation even with little urine. Peritoneal dialysis does not remove volume fast enough for acute pulmonary edema.

CARDIAC TAMPONADE

Tamponade in ESRD may lack classic findings and present with altered mental status, hypotension, dyspnea, edema/weight gain, or intradialytic hypotension. Bedside ultrasound is the best rapid test.

PERICARDITIS

Uremic pericarditis may have a loud/palpable rub and minimal classic ECG changes because inflammation does not usually penetrate myocardium. Dialysis-related pericarditis follows missed/inadequate dialysis or catabolic states. Stable patients need intensive dialysis; worsening effusion or tamponade needs drainage planning.

HEMATOLOGIC COMPLICATIONS

ANEMIA

Anemia is normocytic/normochromic from decreased erythropoietin, dialysis blood loss, phlebotomy, and shortened RBC survival; erythropoietin therapy improves exercise tolerance and quality of life.

COAGULOPATHY

Uremic platelet dysfunction increases GI bleeding, subdural hematoma, liver hematoma, and ocular bleeding risk. Desmopressin 0.3 mcg/kg IV/SC, cryoprecipitate, conjugated estrogen, or tranexamic acid may be used depending on acuity and consultation.

IMMUNE DISORDERS

ESRD impairs leukocyte chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and T-cell activation; dialysis does not fix immune dysfunction and access infections can become life-threatening.

RENAL BONE DISEASE

Hyperphosphatemia and high calcium-phosphate product cause metastatic calcification, pseudogout, skin/finger necrosis, and cardiac/pulmonary calcification. Low-calcium dialysate and phosphate binders are typical therapy.

BETA2-MICROGLOBULIN AMYLOIDOSIS

Dialysis-related amyloid occurs after long dialysis duration, causing GI perforation, bone cysts/pathologic fractures, carpal tunnel syndrome, rotator cuff tears, arthropathy, and higher mortality.

HEMODIALYSIS

TECHNICAL ASPECTS OF HEMODIALYSIS

Hemodialysis uses a filter to create ultrafiltrate, removes blood through large-bore access needles, and controls fluid by pressure gradient and solute by pore size, ultrafiltration, and diffusion. Sessions usually last 3 to 4 hours.

COMPLICATIONS OF VASCULAR ACCESS

Access may be native AV fistula, graft, or tunneled cuffed catheter. Tunneled cuffed catheters should not be removed by pulling. Access complications drive many admissions.

INADEQUATE FLOW

Stenosis or thrombosis causes inadequate flow and loss of bruit/thrill. Treatment within 24 hours may include angiographic clot removal, angioplasty, or alteplase with vascular surgery.

INFECTIONS

Access infection may present with fever, hypotension, leukocytosis, or few local signs. Catheter bacteremia can cause death, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, or epidural abscess. Draw peripheral and catheter cultures; vancomycin is common empiric therapy with gram-negative coverage when needed.

BLEEDING

Access hemorrhage may result from aneurysm, anastomotic rupture, or anticoagulation. Start with direct pressure 5 to 10 minutes and observe if controlled. Escalate to topical thrombin/gelatin, protamine if heparin-related, tourniquets, figure-of-eight suture, vascular surgery, TXA, or massive transfusion when severe. Large-volume access bleeding is a vascular surgery emergency.

ULCERATED HEMODIALYSIS FISTULA

Repeated puncture can ulcerate the fistula wall; a figure-of-eight suture may tear through rather than seal. Control bleeding first, then inspect wall integrity.

VASCULAR ACCESS ANEURYSMS

Aneurysms from repeated puncture are often asymptomatic but can hemorrhage massively. Use proximal/distal control and urgent vascular consultation.

VASCULAR ACCESS PSEUDOANEURYSMS

Pseudoaneurysm follows subcutaneous extravasation from puncture sites; Doppler ultrasound identifies the lesion and vascular surgery should be consulted.

VASCULAR INSUFFICIENCY

Steal syndrome causes exercise pain, nonhealing ulcers, and cool pulseless digits distal to access. Doppler/angiography confirms; treatment is surgical.

HIGH-OUTPUT HEART FAILURE

When more than 20% of cardiac output diverts through access, high-output failure can occur. Branham sign and Doppler flow help diagnose; banding decreases flow.

COMPLICATIONS DURING HEMODIALYSIS

HYPOTENSION

Intradialytic hypotension is common. Causes include excessive ultrafiltration, predialysis volume loss, bleeding, medications, sepsis, ischemia, arrhythmia, and tamponade. Treat by stopping dialysis, Trendelenburg, oral salt or 100-500 mL IV saline; persistent shock needs broader ED evaluation.

DIALYSIS DISEQUILIBRIUM

Rapid solute clearance, especially first dialysis or hypercatabolic states, can create cerebral edema with nausea, vomiting, hypertension, seizure, coma, and death. Stop dialysis and give hypertonic saline or mannitol.

AIR EMBOLISM

Air embolism causes dyspnea, chest tightness, neurologic symptoms, loss of consciousness, hypotension, or arrest. Clamp venous line, place supine, and give 100% oxygen; consider aspiration, anticoagulation, steroids, or hyperbaric oxygen case-by-case.

ELECTROLYTE DISTURBANCES

Dialysate mixing errors can cause osmolar shifts and hemolysis, but hyperkalemia is the common ED electrolyte emergency. Hypoglycemia also occurs from drugs, malnutrition, and sepsis.

ED EVALUATION OF HEMODIALYSIS PATIENTS

Ask cause of ESRD, dialysis schedule, missed sessions, urine production, dry weight, baseline labs/vitals, weight gain, intradialytic hypotension timing, functioning access, uremic symptoms, and retained native kidneys. Examine access, volume, heart, lungs, neurologic status, rectal bleeding, and signs of ischemia or pseudoaneurysm.

PERITONEAL DIALYSIS

TECHNICAL ASPECTS OF PERITONEAL DIALYSIS

PD uses the peritoneal membrane as the blood-dialysate interface. Glucose concentration drives ultrafiltration; solutes diffuse down concentration gradients. Continuous ambulatory PD uses exchanges throughout the day, and continuous cyclic PD performs exchanges at night.

COMPLICATIONS OF CONTINUOUS AMBULATORY PERITONEAL DIALYSIS

PERITONITIS

PD peritonitis presents with fever, abdominal pain, rebound tenderness, and cloudy effluent. Confirm with Gram stain, cell count, and culture; WBC is usually >100/mm3 with >50% neutrophils. Treat with rapid exchanges, heparin in dialysate, and intraperitoneal antibiotics. Parenteral antibiotics are not the usual route for uncomplicated PD peritonitis.

ABDOMINAL WALL COMPLICATIONS

PD ACCESS SITE COMPLICATIONS

Exit-site infections cause pain, erythema, swelling, or discharge around the catheter, commonly S. aureus or Pseudomonas. Oral cephalosporin or ciprofloxacin may be used with next-day PD center follow-up. Pericatheter hernias have high incarceration risk and often need early repair.

ED EVALUATION OF PERITONEAL DIALYSIS PATIENTS

Ask cause of ESRD, PD type, exchange concentration/number, recent complications, baseline weight/labs/vitals, uremic symptoms, native kidneys, and urine output. Examine abdomen for hernia/rebound, catheter exit skin, and tunnel tenderness.

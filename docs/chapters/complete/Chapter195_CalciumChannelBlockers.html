Tintinalli Chapter 195

Calcium Channel Blockers

CCB poisoning is cardiogenic and vasodilatory shock plus hyperglycemia: calcium, HDI, vasopressors, glucagon, lipid rescue, and ECMO when needed.

MCQ 28Source images 5Crop QA PASS

Overview

Calcium channel blockers inhibit L-type calcium channels in myocardium, conduction tissue, vascular smooth muscle, and pancreatic beta cells. In overdose, selectivity is lost and patients develop hypotension, bradycardia/AV block, shock, and hyperglycemia.

Extended-release products can delay toxicity for 6-12 hours or longer. Children can develop severe toxicity after small ingestions, and adults on chronic therapy may become toxic after relatively modest dose increases.

Pharmacology

Phenylalkylamines such as verapamil are the most negative inotropic; benzothiazepines such as diltiazem slow nodal conduction; dihydropyridines are more vascular selective at therapeutic doses but lose selectivity in overdose.

Clinical Features and Diagnosis

Life-threatening findings are an extension of therapeutic effects: decreased cardiac output and peripheral vasodilation. Pulmonary edema may occur when large crystalloid volumes are given. CNS depression, seizure, delirium, and coma can reflect hypoperfusion rather than primary CNS poisoning.

Diagnosis is clinical. Check ECG, glucose, electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, renal function, lactate/acid-base status, and coingestants. Hyperglycemia is common because CCBs inhibit insulin release.

Treatment

Initial care: airway as needed, ECG monitoring, IV access, glucose and electrolyte checks, cautious crystalloid, calcium salts, vasopressors, and early toxicology support. Activated charcoal is reasonable early if the airway is protected; whole-bowel irrigation can be considered for large extended-release ingestion.

Calcium can transiently improve contractility and conduction. Vasopressors support perfusion. Glucagon may help but is less reliable than in beta-blocker toxicity. Lipid emulsion and extracorporeal circulatory support are rescue options for refractory shock.

Layered shock therapy

High-Dose Insulin and Severe Shock

High-dose insulin therapy is a core treatment for myocardial depression in severe CCB poisoning. It improves myocardial carbohydrate utilization and inotropy. Monitor glucose every 15-20 minutes early and potassium closely; dextrose support can be required long after shock improves.

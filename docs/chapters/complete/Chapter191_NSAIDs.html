Overview: Therapeutic Harm vs Acute Overdose

NSAIDs are common analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory drugs. Acute overdose is often benign, but the population burden comes from therapeutic-dose toxicity: GI bleeding, renal injury, hypertension, and cardiovascular events. ED risk stratification separates stable, asymptomatic overdose from chronic toxicity or massive ingestion.

Most early acute symptoms are abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting within 4 hours. Severe findings such as coma, seizure, shock, metabolic acidosis, hepatic injury, or acute kidney injury imply massive ingestion or a high-risk agent.

Toxicity at Therapeutic Doses

COX inhibition reduces protective prostaglandins. GI toxicity ranges from dyspepsia to ulceration and bleeding. Renal toxicity is worse in dehydration, CKD, heart failure, cirrhosis, older age, and ACEi/ARB/diuretic use. NSAIDs also interact with anticoagulants and may blunt aspirin's antiplatelet effect.

Toxicity with Acute Overdose

Most acute overdoses produce limited symptoms. Severe toxicity can include altered mental status, coma, seizures, hypotension, shock, dysrhythmias, hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, renal injury, hepatic injury, pancreatitis, and increased anion gap metabolic acidosis. Mefenamic acid is the classic NSAID seizure association.

Board trap: NSAID levels do not correlate with toxicity or outcomes. Treat the patient, check coingestions, and observe clinically.

Treatment

Treatment is supportive. Symptomatic patients get airway management, fluids, vasopressors if needed, benzodiazepines for seizures, electrolyte correction, and evaluation for coingestions. Activated charcoal is reasonable for early significant ingestion when the airway is protected. Hemodialysis, hemoperfusion, and urine alkalinization do not enhance NSAID elimination because NSAIDs are highly protein bound.

Disposition

Asymptomatic patients with no concerning coingestion can usually be observed for 4 hours and discharged if stable. Admit patients with altered mental status, abnormal vitals, seizures, acidosis, electrolyte abnormalities, hepatic injury, renal failure, shock, or persistent symptoms. Intentional ingestion requires psychiatric evaluation after medical clearance.

Key Points

Acute often benign
  • Observe stable asymptomatic patients for 4 hours.
Therapeutic harm matters
  • GI bleed and renal injury dominate real-world morbidity.
No drug level
  • Serum NSAID levels do not guide management.
No enhanced elimination
  • Protein binding limits dialysis/alkalinization.

1. Introduction & Epidemiology

Tintinalli Ch.190 p.1253

Rosen vs Tintinalli: Rosen adds ED-facing nuance for acetaminophen toxicity while keeping the Tintinalli treatment spine.

  • Acetaminophen (paracetamol, APAP) — most commonly used analgesic/antipyretic worldwide.
  • Most common cause of acute liver failure (ALF) in the US — ~50% of all ALF cases.
  • ~50,000 ED visits annually in US; ~150 deaths/year.
  • Two patterns of toxicity:
    • Acute single ingestion (most common in suicide attempts).
    • Repeated supratherapeutic ingestion (RSTI / staggered/chronic) — often unintentional from combination products (e.g., narcotic + APAP); harder to assess with nomogram.
  • Toxic single dose: ≥150 mg/kg (or ≥7.5 g in adult, ≥200 mg/kg in child).
Pearl — combination products: Patients with chronic pain or recent surgery may unknowingly exceed safe APAP doses through combination products (Percocet, Vicodin, NyQuil). Always ask about all pain meds + OTC combinations.

2. Pharmacology & Mechanism of Toxicity

Tintinalli p.1253–1254

Pharmacokinetics

  • Rapid absorption, peak serum level 1–2 h post-ingestion (immediate-release); 4 h or longer with extended-release or with co-ingestants that slow GI motility (opioids, anticholinergics).
  • Therapeutic half-life: 2–4 h. Prolonged in toxicity (>4 h).
  • Hepatic metabolism via 3 pathways:
    • Glucuronidation (~60%) — non-toxic.
    • Sulfation (~30%) — non-toxic.
    • CYP2E1 oxidation (~5%) → forms toxic metabolite NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine).

NAPQI Toxicity Pathway

  • NAPQI is normally rapidly conjugated by glutathione → non-toxic mercapturate.
  • In overdose: glucuronidation/sulfation pathways saturated → ↑ CYP2E1 metabolism → ↑↑ NAPQI.
  • Glutathione stores depleted (when ≥70% used → toxicity begins).
  • Free NAPQI binds covalently to hepatocyte proteins → centrilobular hepatocellular necrosis (zone III, around central vein).

Risk factors for enhanced toxicity

  • Chronic alcohol use — induces CYP2E1, depletes glutathione (toxicity at lower doses).
  • Malnutrition, anorexia, prolonged fasting — reduced glutathione stores.
  • Chronic liver disease.
  • CYP-inducing drugs (rifampin, phenytoin, phenobarbital, INH, carbamazepine).
  • Pediatric and elderly — variable risk.

3. Clinical Stages of APAP Toxicity

Tintinalli p.1254
📚 Table 190-1 — Clinical Stages of Acute Acetaminophen Toxicity: timing, clinical manifestations, lab abnormalities by stage (Tintinalli p.1254)
StageTime post-ingestionClinical features
Stage I0.5–24 hOften asymptomatic or non-specific: nausea, vomiting, malaise, diaphoresis, pallor. Labs: normal initially.
Stage II24–72 h"Quiescent phase" — symptoms may improve. Lab evidence of hepatic injury appears: ↑ AST/ALT (often very high), ↑ INR, RUQ pain, hepatomegaly, oliguria.
Stage III72–96 hPeak hepatotoxicity: jaundice, encephalopathy, coagulopathy (INR >5), AKI, hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis. ALT often >10,000 IU/L. Death from fulminant hepatic failure typically here.
Stage IV4 days – 2 weeksRecovery if survives. Hepatic regeneration (no chronic liver damage); LFTs normalize over 5–7 days.
⚠️ Key trap — Stage I deception: Initial 24 h often appears benign (asymptomatic or just nausea). Do NOT be reassured by absence of symptoms. Always obtain APAP level and determine treatment based on level + time, not on symptoms.

Pathognomonic lab findings of severe APAP toxicity (Stage III)

  • AST/ALT often >10,000 IU/L (massively elevated, more than other causes of acute liver injury).
  • INR/PT prolonged out of proportion to AST/ALT.
  • Lactic acidosis.
  • Hypoglycemia.
  • Renal injury (~25% of severe cases — direct NAPQI tubular toxicity, not just hepatorenal).

4. Diagnosis & Workup

Tintinalli p.1254

Initial labs (every suspected APAP overdose)

  • APAP serum level — at ≥4 hours post-ingestion (earlier levels not interpretable on nomogram).
  • Salicylate level — common co-ingestant; affects management.
  • Basic chemistry panel — BUN, Cr, glucose, electrolytes.
  • LFTs — AST, ALT, total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase.
  • Coagulation panel — PT/INR, PTT.
  • ABG/lactate — assess acidosis.
  • Urinalysis — phosphate, glucose, protein.
  • β-hCG in women of childbearing age.
  • ECG — co-ingestion screening (TCA, salicylate, etc.).
Pearl — APAP level timing:
• Levels obtained before 4 h post-ingestion are not interpretable on Rumack-Matthew (because absorption may not be complete).
• If ingestion time unknown OR mixed extended-release/coingestant: get level immediately, then a second level 4 h later; treat empirically with NAC if delayed.

5. Rumack-Matthew Nomogram

Tintinalli Fig 190-2 p.1255
📚 Fig 190-2 — Rumack-Matthew nomogram. Plot APAP serum level vs hours post-ingestion. Above the line ("Possible Toxicity") = treat with NAC (Tintinalli p.1255)

Treatment threshold

  • 200 µg/mL at 4 h (1300 µmol/L) — original "200 line".
  • 150 µg/mL at 4 h (1000 µmol/L) — currently used "150 line" (US treatment line, more conservative).
  • Halves every ~4 hours: 50 at 12 h, 25 at 16 h, 12.5 at 20 h, ~6 at 24 h.
  • Plot APAP level above this line → start NAC.

Limitations of the nomogram

  • Single acute ingestion only — known time of ingestion required.
  • 4–24 hour window — not valid before 4 h or after 24 h.
  • NOT applicable to:
    • Repeated supratherapeutic ingestion (RSTI / chronic / staggered).
    • Extended-release products (acetaminophen ER) — get a 2nd level 4 h later.
    • Co-ingestion with anticholinergic / opioid (delayed gastric emptying).
    • Unknown time of ingestion → treat empirically.
Pearl — When NOT to use nomogram: any uncertainty about time, dose, or formulation → empirically treat with NAC. The nomogram has saved many lives but has its limitations.

6. GI Decontamination

Tintinalli p.1255
  • Activated charcoal 1 g/kg PO within 1–2 h post-ingestion (extended to 4 h for very large ingestions or extended-release).
  • Charcoal binds APAP effectively in the gut.
  • Does NOT interfere with PO NAC — give charcoal first if both indicated.
  • Gastric lavage and whole-bowel irrigation generally NOT indicated.
  • Airway must be protected if obtunded.

7. N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) — The Antidote

Tintinalli Table 190-2 p.1256

Mechanism

  • Glutathione precursor → replenishes hepatic glutathione stores.
  • Direct conjugation with NAPQI → non-toxic conjugate.
  • May provide supportive antioxidant + improve microcirculation in established hepatotoxicity.
  • Maximum effectiveness within 8 h of ingestion — reduces hepatotoxicity to <1%.
  • Still beneficial after 8 h (less effective but still indicated up to 24 h or even later if toxicity already established).

Indications for NAC

  1. APAP level above the Rumack-Matthew treatment line (4–24 h).
  2. Time of ingestion unknown but APAP detectable + suggestive history.
  3. Single dose >150 mg/kg + APAP level pending if >8 h delay.
  4. Repeated supratherapeutic ingestion + AST/ALT elevated or APAP detectable.
  5. Suspected APAP + signs of hepatotoxicity (transaminitis), regardless of APAP level.
  6. Pregnancy with significant APAP overdose.

Side effects

  • PO: nausea/vomiting (common — sulfur smell), diarrhea. Antiemetics + diluted with juice.

  • True anaphylaxis very rare; epinephrine if severe.

8. Treatment Algorithm by Time Post-Ingestion

Tintinalli Fig 190-3 p.1257
📚 Fig 190-3 — Treatment guidelines for APAP ingestion by time post-ingestion (Tintinalli p.1257)

Approach by time

TimeApproach
<4 hActivated charcoal if no contraindications. Get APAP level at 4 h. Plot on nomogram.
4–8 hGet APAP level immediately. Plot on nomogram. Start NAC if above line. Decontamination if recent.
8–24 hGet APAP + LFTs immediately. Start NAC empirically while awaiting level (don't delay if level pending). Stop NAC if APAP below line.
>24 h or unknown timeGet APAP + LFTs. Start NAC if APAP >10 µg/mL OR AST/ALT elevated OR clinical concern. Continue until clinically improved + INR/LFTs normalizing.
⚠️ Critical pearl: Optimal NAC effectiveness is within 8 h of ingestion. If presentation is >8 h, START NAC EMPIRICALLY while waiting for the APAP level. Do not delay treatment.

Stopping NAC criteria

  • NAC can be stopped if at end of standard course AND:
    • APAP undetectable (or below treatment threshold).
    • AST/ALT trending down.
    • INR <2 (improving).
    • Patient clinically improved.
  • Continue NAC beyond standard course if any criteria not met.

9. King's College Criteria — Liver Transplantation

Tintinalli p.1257

Predicts mortality from acute liver failure due to APAP — used to identify candidates for emergency liver transplantation. Refer to a transplant center if:

King's College Criteria (APAP-induced ALF)
Either:
• Arterial pH <7.30 after fluid resuscitation
OR all three of:
• PT >100 s (INR >6.5)
• Creatinine >3.4 mg/dL (>300 µmol/L)
• Grade III or IV hepatic encephalopathy
⚠️ Pearl — when to call the transplant team: any one of {pH <7.30, INR >6.5, Cr >3.4, encephalopathy grade III–IV} = high-mortality. Get transplant team involved EARLY — don't wait for all four.

Other prognostic markers

  • Lactate >3.5 at 4 h or >3.0 at 12 h post-resuscitation = poor prognosis.
  • Phosphate >3.7 mg/dL at 48–96 h = poor prognosis (hepatic regeneration uses phosphate).
  • Continue NAC until INR <2 and clinical improvement.

10. Special Populations & Specific Scenarios

Tintinalli p.1255–1257

Pregnancy

  • NAC is safe and indicated in pregnancy (FDA Category B).
  • APAP crosses placenta and fetal liver also vulnerable to NAPQI.
  • Treat per standard nomogram + NAC protocol.
  • IV NAC may be preferred in pregnancy due to less PO smell/nausea.

Pediatric

  • Same nomogram + NAC protocol.
  • IV pediatric dosing volume-adjusted (see Table 190-2).
  • Children <5 years often have favorable outcomes (better glutathione regeneration).

Repeated supratherapeutic ingestion (RSTI / staggered)

  • Nomogram is NOT applicable.
  • Treat with NAC if:
    • Total dose >150 mg/kg in 24 h, OR >10 g/24 h, OR >6 g/day for 48 h.
    • APAP >10 µg/mL (regardless of time).
    • AST/ALT elevated.
  • RSTI patients may have higher mortality than acute single ingestion despite lower peak APAP levels.

Extended-release acetaminophen

  • Get a level immediately AND a second level 4 h later (or 4–6 h after ingestion).
  • Treat if EITHER level is above the nomogram line.
  • If both levels below line and patient asymptomatic, observation may be sufficient.

Co-ingestion with opioids/anticholinergics

  • Delayed gastric emptying → delayed peak APAP level.
  • Get serial levels (4 h, 8 h) — treat if either is above line.

Chronic alcohol use disorder

  • Increased risk at lower doses (CYP2E1 induction + glutathione depletion).
  • Some authors recommend lower nomogram threshold (100 µg/mL line) in alcoholics — controversial but be aggressive in NAC initiation.

11. Disposition

Tintinalli p.1257
  • Discharge home: APAP level below treatment line at 4 h + asymptomatic + no signs of hepatic injury + reliable disposition + psychiatric eval if intentional.
  • Admission for NAC: APAP toxicity by nomogram or empiric treatment. Continue inpatient until completion of NAC course + LFTs normalizing + INR <2.
  • ICU: hepatic encephalopathy, coagulopathy with bleeding, AKI requiring dialysis, hemodynamic instability, fulminant hepatic failure.
  • Transfer to liver transplant center: any King's College criteria met, INR >3, encephalopathy, or rapidly worsening LFTs/coagulation.
  • Psychiatric consultation for intentional ingestions before discharge.

⚕️ ATLS 11th — APAP Overdose in Trauma

ATLS
  • APAP overdose is a non-traumatic emergency but may co-exist with trauma (suicide attempts, motor vehicle collisions in poisoned drivers).
  • Always check APAP level in any unconscious or polypharmacy overdose patient — easily missed.
  • Perfuse aggressively — avoid LR if active hepatic dysfunction (impaired lactate metabolism); use NS + balanced crystalloid.
  • Coagulopathy from hepatic failure — replace fibrinogen, FFP, platelets per trauma protocols.
  • Avoid succinylcholine in suspected hepatorenal failure (slower clearance + metabolic effects); rocuronium preferred.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy → consider ICP monitoring, mannitol/HTS for elevated ICP, neurosurgery consult.

📗 Rosen's EM 10th — Acetaminophen Toxicity

Rosen source
Rosen's Ch.146
  • Aligns with Tintinalli — same nomogram (US 150 line) and NAC protocols.
  • Rosen's emphasizes: liberal NAC use; the threshold for NAC initiation is low because consequences of missed APAP toxicity are severe.
  • "Free AST/ALT" trending — a downtrend suggests recovery; uptrend with INR worsening suggests progression.
  • RSTI — Rosen's notes higher mortality than acute despite lower peak APAP; have low threshold for NAC.
  • Pregnancy — both authors recommend NAC; IV preferred to minimize fetal smell exposure (anecdotal).
  • Liver transplant criteria — King's College + lactate-based scoring; involve transplant team early.
  • Extracorporeal removal (hemodialysis) reserved for severe cases with sustained APAP >1000 µg/mL, metabolic acidosis, AMS, encephalopathy.

🔑 Key Points (High-Yield)

  1. NAPQI = toxic metabolite (CYP2E1); depletes glutathione → centrilobular hepatocyte necrosis.
  2. Toxic dose: >150 mg/kg or >7.5 g in adults.
  3. 4 stages: I (0–24h, asymptomatic), II (24–72h, hepatic injury appears), III (72–96h, peak — fulminant failure), IV (recovery).
  4. Rumack-Matthew nomogram: APAP level at ≥4h vs time. US 150 line → start NAC if at/above.
  5. NOT interpretable on nomogram: <4h, RSTI/staggered, ER products, co-ingest opioids/anticholinergics, unknown time.
  6. NAC IV (adult): 150 mg/kg load over 60 min → 50 mg/kg over 4h → 100 mg/kg over 16h (total 21h).
  7. NAC PO: 140 mg/kg load → 70 mg/kg q4h × 17 doses (72h).
  8. NAC anaphylactoid reactions ~10–17% with IV — slow + antihistamine; NOT true anaphylaxis.
  9. Maximum NAC effect within 8 h of ingestion (reduces hepatotoxicity to <1%).
  10. If presentation >8h or unknown time: start NAC empirically + send level + LFTs.
  11. Activated charcoal within 1–2 h of ingestion — does NOT interfere with NAC.
  12. King's College criteria: pH <7.30 OR all three (PT >100/INR >6.5, Cr >3.4, encephalopathy III/IV) → liver transplant.
  13. Chronic alcoholics: lower toxicity threshold; consider 100 µg/mL line.
  14. Pregnancy: NAC safe (Category B); IV often preferred.
  15. Massive transaminitis (AST/ALT >10,000) is characteristic of severe APAP toxicity.

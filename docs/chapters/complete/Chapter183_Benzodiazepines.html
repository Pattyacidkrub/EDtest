Toxicology Chapter 183

Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepine overdose is usually a supportive-care chapter: protect ventilation, identify coingestants, and do not reflexively give flumazenil.

Board frame: isolated benzodiazepines are often benign; mixed overdoses and withdrawal are not.

Pharmacology and Kinetics

Benzodiazepines bind the GABA-A receptor and increase the frequency of chloride-channel opening. This produces anxiolysis, sedation, amnesia, anticonvulsant activity, and muscle relaxation. Toxicity is usually CNS depression, but opioids, ethanol, barbiturates, and other sedatives can convert a quiet overdose into respiratory failure.

Lipid solubility explains rapid CNS entry and redistribution; metabolism explains duration. Agents with long half-lives or active metabolites can cause prolonged sedation, especially in older adults, liver disease, and repeated dosing.

Clinical Features

Typical isolated overdose causes somnolence, ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus, and impaired coordination. Vital signs are often preserved, but respiratory depression can occur with large doses or coingestants. Severe hypotension, marked hypoxia, hyperthermia, clonus, wide QRS, or refractory shock should trigger a search beyond isolated benzodiazepines.

Expected

Drowsiness, slurred speech, ataxia, mild respiratory depression.

Danger

Opioids/ethanol and aspiration risk, especially with deep coma.

Not typical

Severe hyperthermia, clonus, wide QRS, or profound metabolic acidosis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical. Check glucose, oxygenation/ventilation, ECG, acetaminophen/salicylate/ethanol when appropriate, pregnancy testing when relevant, and evaluate for trauma or infection when the story is incomplete. A urine benzodiazepine screen can support exposure but does not prove causality or severity.

Standard immunoassays can miss lorazepam, clonazepam, midazolam, alprazolam, or designer benzodiazepines depending on assay design. Do not use a negative screen to overrule a compatible clinical course.

Anchor check: coma that is too deep, too unstable, or too prolonged should be treated as mixed/unknown until proven otherwise.

Treatment

Supportive care is the treatment spine. Place the patient on cardiorespiratory and pulse-oximetry monitoring, support airway position, give oxygen, and ventilate or intubate when protective reflexes or ventilation are inadequate. Treat hypoglycemia and look for coingestants.

Activated charcoal is usually not indicated for isolated benzodiazepine ingestion because aspiration risk often outweighs benefit. Hemodialysis and hemoperfusion are ineffective for benzodiazepine removal.

Flumazenil

Flumazenil is a competitive benzodiazepine antagonist. It can reverse iatrogenic procedural sedation or selected accidental isolated exposures in non-habituated patients. It is not a routine coma antidote because it can precipitate seizures, withdrawal, and dysrhythmias in mixed or dependent patients.

Benzodiazepine Withdrawal

Dependence can develop after prolonged use, especially high-dose use. Abrupt cessation can produce anxiety, tremor, insomnia, tachycardia, hypertension, hallucinations, delirium, and seizures. Severe withdrawal needs monitored treatment, usually with reinstatement of a benzodiazepine and a gradual taper.

Disposition

Patients with mild, improving isolated overdose can often be observed until mental status and ventilation are stable. Admit patients with persistent respiratory depression, aspiration, recurrent sedation after antagonist use, unstable vital signs, significant coingestants, self-harm risk, or severe withdrawal.

Intentional ingestion requires psychiatric evaluation after medical clearance. Chronic users need a safe taper/withdrawal plan before discharge.

Beta-Blockers

Bradycardic shock chapter: glucagon, high-dose insulin, vasopressors, sodium bicarbonate, pacing, and ECMO when needed.

MCQ 28Source images 6Crop QA PASS

Overview

Beta-blocker overdose primarily injures the cardiovascular system: bradycardia, hypotension, conduction delay, shock, and decreased contractility. Some agents add CNS toxicity, bronchospasm, sodium-channel blockade, or potassium-channel blockade.

Management is physiology-driven: identify QRS widening, QT prolongation, contractility failure, low systemic vascular resistance, and bradycardia, then layer therapies rather than waiting for one perfect antidote.

Pharmacology and High-Risk Agents

Beta receptors regulate myocardium, bronchial smooth muscle, kidney renin release, liver metabolism, and vascular tone. Toxicity severity depends on receptor selectivity, lipid solubility, protein binding, sodium-channel blockade, partial agonism, formulation, and half-life.

Clinical Features

Symptoms often develop within 1-4 hours for regular-release products, but sustained-release products and coingestants can delay onset. Severe cases show bradycardia, hypotension, conduction delay, cardiogenic shock, depressed mental status, seizure, bronchospasm, and respiratory arrest.

Propranolol is high-risk because it is lipophilic and can cause CNS depression, seizures, and sodium-channel blockade. Sotalol is different because it can prolong QT and cause torsades de pointes.

Diagnosis and Differential

Diagnosis is clinical: history, medication list, timing/formulation, ECG, glucose, renal function, electrolytes, lactate/acid-base status, and bedside cardiac ultrasound when shock is present. Drug levels are rarely useful in real-time ED care.

Treatment

Start with resuscitation, IV access, ECG monitoring, airway support when needed, fluids if appropriate, and early poison center consultation. Activated charcoal may be considered within 1 hour if the airway is protected; whole-bowel irrigation may be considered for large extended-release ingestion.

Glucagon is a first-line agent for acute beta-blocker-induced bradycardia and hypotension. High-dose insulin therapy improves myocardial contractility and metabolism. Adrenergic agonists and vasopressors are often needed, and therapy is tailored to contractility, SVR, and rhythm.

For QRS widening, consider sodium bicarbonate. For sotalol-related QT prolongation/torsades, magnesium and overdrive pacing may be needed. Refractory shock can require intra-aortic balloon pump, ECMO, or other mechanical circulatory support.

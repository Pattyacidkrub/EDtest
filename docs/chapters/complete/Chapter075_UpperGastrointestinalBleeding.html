Gastrointestinal Disorders

Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Upper GI bleeding is bleeding proximal to the ligament of Treitz. The ED job is not to name the final lesion immediately; it is to recognize shock, resuscitate, risk-stratify, start source-directed therapy, and get endoscopy or rescue consultation moving.

Board trap: Hematochezia can be a brisk upper GI bleed when the patient is unstable.

UPPER GASTROINTESTINAL BLEEDING

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

Incidence varies by country, H. pylori prevalence, NSAID/aspirin use, ulcer-protective prescribing, age, and comorbidity. Older age, recurrent hemorrhage, organ-system disease, anticoagulants, and portal hypertension raise morbidity and mortality.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

PEPTIC ULCER DISEASE

Peptic ulcer disease remains a major cause even though H. pylori treatment and PPI use have reduced severe ulcer bleeding. NSAIDs, aspirin, smoking, anticoagulation, and prior ulcer history increase risk. Epigastric pain plus melena or hematemesis should keep ulcer bleeding high on the list.

EROSIVE GASTRITIS AND ESOPHAGITIS

Alcohol, salicylates, NSAIDs, infection, radiation, caustics, and physiologic stress can erode mucosa. Bleeding may be slower than variceal bleeding but can still destabilize vulnerable patients.

ESOPHAGEAL AND GASTRIC VARICES

Portal hypertension creates collateral vessels that can bleed massively. Look for cirrhosis, ascites, jaundice, spider angiomas, encephalopathy, prior banding, or TIPS history. Variceal suspicion changes ED therapy: add octreotide and antibiotics early.

MALLORY-WEISS SYNDROME

A mucosal tear near the gastroesophageal junction follows retching, vomiting, coughing, seizures, binge alcohol use, DKA, chemotherapy, or other abrupt pressure events. Many stop spontaneously, but persistent bleeding still needs endoscopic management.

DIEULAFOY LESIONS

Dieulafoy lesions are protruding submucosal arteries, often near the lesser curvature, that can cause intermittent but massive bleeding. Patients may have repeated nondiagnostic evaluations before the lesion is found.

OTHER CAUSES

Arteriovenous malformations, malignancy, unusual lesions, swallowed blood, and aortoenteric fistula can mimic common UGIB. A prior aortic graft plus GI bleeding is an exsanguination warning until proven otherwise.

DIAGNOSIS

HISTORY

Ask about hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena, hematochezia, vomiting/retching, abdominal pain, prior GI bleed, ulcer disease, cirrhosis, NSAIDs, aspirin, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, alcohol, aortic graft, weight loss, bismuth/iron, and foods that mimic blood. Symptoms of hypoperfusion, confusion, syncope, weakness, dyspnea, or atypical ACS symptoms indicate severity.

PHYSICAL EXAMINATION

Vital signs may be deceptively normal early. Assess shock index, mental status, perfusion, abdominal tenderness/peritonitis, liver disease stigmata, ascites, petechiae/purpura, and rectal findings. Visual inspection of emesis or NG aspirate for bloody, maroon, or coffee-ground material is the most reliable way to diagnose upper GI bleeding in the ED.

LABORATORY TESTING

Obtain CBC, type and screen/crossmatch, electrolytes, BUN/creatinine, glucose, coagulation studies, platelet count, liver tests when indicated, lactate for occult shock, and ECG/troponin when coronary disease or demand ischemia is possible. BUN:creatinine elevation may support an upper source but cannot rule it in or out.

NASOGASTRIC LAVAGE

Routine NG placement is not required. A negative aspirate does not exclude upper GI bleeding because bleeding may be intermittent, pyloric spasm may prevent reflux, or duodenal blood may not enter the stomach. Positive bloody, maroon, or coffee-ground aspirate supports UGIB and may improve endoscopic visualization in selected cases.

RISK STRATIFICATION

Risk stratification is clinical. Very low-risk patients have younger age, no major comorbidities, no hematemesis, negative NG aspirate, stability, and normal labs; high-risk patients have advanced age, comorbidities, hematemesis/melena, positive aspirate, instability, or abnormal labs. Rosen adds Glasgow-Blatchford scoring to estimate intervention/admission risk.

TREATMENT

Initial management is stabilization. Place two large-bore IVs, prepare blood, activate massive transfusion when needed, monitor continuously, correct hypoxia, and avoid intubation-induced collapse by resuscitating before induction when possible. Do not wait for the first hemoglobin to fall before treating obvious hemorrhagic shock.

BLOOD TRANSFUSIONS

Use massive transfusion for severe active hemorrhage. In less severe cases, individualize transfusion; a restrictive threshold around hemoglobin 7 g/dL is preferred for most, with higher targets for older patients, active ischemia, or important comorbidities.

COAGULOPATHY

Reverse life-threatening anticoagulant-associated bleeding when benefits exceed thrombotic risk. Platelets under 50,000/µL and elevated INR in severe bleeding are high-risk. Reversal should occur alongside, not instead of, endoscopy planning.

PROTON PUMP INHIBITORS

High-dose PPI therapy is used for suspected nonvariceal ulcer bleeding because neutral gastric pH supports clot formation. Tintinalli lists omeprazole 80 mg IV bolus then 8 mg/h infusion.

SOMATOSTATIN ANALOGUES/OCTREOTIDE

Octreotide reduces splanchnic blood flow and is most important when varices are possible. Give a 50 mcg bolus followed by 25-50 mcg/h infusion; do not delay endoscopy.

ANTIBIOTICS

Cirrhotic patients with acute upper GI bleeding need early prophylactic antibiotics. Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV or ceftriaxone 1 g IV reduces infection, rebleeding, hospitalization, and mortality.

PROMOTILITY AGENTS

Erythromycin or metoclopramide may improve endoscopic visualization when the stomach likely contains large blood/clot burden. This is a visualization aid, not definitive hemostasis.

ENDOSCOPY

Endoscopy is the diagnostic and therapeutic study of choice. Timing depends on severity: unstable patients need early endoscopy after resuscitation, and most stable hospitalized patients undergo endoscopy within 12-36 hours. Endoscopic therapy may include ligation, sclerotherapy, clips, thermocoagulation, injection, or combination therapy.

BALLOON TAMPONADE

Balloon tamponade is a temporary bridge for life-threatening presumed variceal bleeding when endoscopy is unavailable or delayed. It has serious complications and should be used only as a short-term rescue while arranging definitive care.

SURGERY

Patients who fail pharmacologic and endoscopic therapy, or who remain unstable with uncontrolled bleeding, need urgent GI, surgery, and/or interventional radiology consultation. Options depend on source: shunts for variceal bleeding, devascularization, or ulcer-directed surgery such as vessel ligation or gastrectomy in selected cases.

DISPOSITION AND FOLLOW-UP

Significant upper GI bleeding requires admission; unstable or actively bleeding patients need ICU-level care. Very low-risk patients may be observed or discharged only when stable, with reassuring labs, no ongoing bleeding, and reliable urgent outpatient follow-up. Disposition is based on trajectory and risk, not just one normal vital-sign set.

Chapter 196: Antihypertensives

Most non-CCB, non-beta-blocker antihypertensive overdoses are managed by airway, monitoring, fluids, ECG, electrolytes, and direct vasopressor support when perfusion fails. The dangerous exceptions are pattern-based: clonidine-like CNS depression, ACEI/ARB angioedema, electrolyte shifts from diuretics, and nitroprusside cyanide/thiocyanate toxicity.

Initial ED Approach

Start the same way for almost every case: secure the airway when mental status or swelling threatens ventilation, place the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring, obtain an ECG, establish IV access, and check glucose/electrolytes. Hypotension gets isotonic crystalloid first. If shock persists, use a direct-acting vasopressor such as norepinephrine or phenylephrine rather than relying on indirect catecholamine release.

Charcoal window. Activated charcoal is reasonable within the first hour only when aspiration risk is low.
Do not miss co-ingestants. CCBs and beta-blockers live in nearby chapters because their overdose physiology and rescue therapies are different.

Class Map From Tintinalli

The table belongs here because it is the recognition map, not a drug-dose appendix. Use it to connect each medication class to its expected clinical presentation and immediate management hazard.

Diuretics

Diuretic toxicity is mainly volume depletion plus electrolyte disorder. Thiazides can produce hypovolemia, hypokalemia, hypercalcemia, and glucose intolerance at higher doses. Loop diuretics can cause hypovolemia with hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia. Potassium-sparing agents such as spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride, and triamterene shift the concern toward hyperkalemia.

On ECG, peaked T waves or QRS widening should push hyperkalemia treatment; QT prolongation should make you check potassium, magnesium, and calcium. Treatment is plasma-volume restoration plus standard electrolyte correction. Diuretics can also amplify lithium and digoxin risk by changing renal clearance or potassium balance.

Sympatholytics

Alpha-1 blockers such as prazosin, terazosin, doxazosin, and tamsulosin cause vasodilation and orthostatic hypotension. Lay the patient supine, give crystalloid, and if still hypotensive choose an alpha-1 agonist logic such as phenylephrine.

Clonidine, guanfacine, guanabenz, oxymetazoline, and tetrahydrozoline can create an opioid-like toxidrome: bradycardia, hypotension, miosis, CNS depression, and sometimes apnea. Support ventilation first. Naloxone is variable in adults but Rosen notes pediatric clonidine somnolence may respond to larger bolus dosing when opioid dependence is not a concern.

ACE Inhibitors and ARBs

ACE inhibitor and ARB overdose rarely causes dramatic isolated shock, but renal insufficiency and aldosterone suppression can produce hyperkalemia. Treat hypotension supportively with crystalloid and vasopressors as needed. ARBs end in -artan; ACE inhibitors end in -pril.

The high-risk presentation is angioedema. Absence of urticaria supports bradykinin-mediated disease rather than routine allergic urticaria. Tongue, floor-of-mouth, voice, laryngeal, or respiratory symptoms require early skilled airway planning. Epinephrine, antihistamines, and steroids are often given but have no proven benefit for ACEI-mediated bradykinin angioedema; targeted options may include icatibant, C1 esterase inhibitor, or FFP depending on local practice and severity.

Vasodilators

Hydralazine and minoxidil cause arteriolar vasodilation with reflex tachycardia. Hypotension usually gets crystalloid; pressors are used carefully because tachycardia can worsen myocardial oxygen demand. Chronic hydralazine can cause a lupus-like syndrome.

Nitroprusside is different: it has a very fast onset and offset, so hypotension usually improves after stopping the infusion and positioning/fluids. Prolonged or high-rate infusion can generate cyanide and later thiocyanate toxicity. Confusion, lactic acidosis, and cardiovascular collapse should trigger stop nitroprusside and give sodium thiosulfate when cyanide toxicity is suspected. Fenoldopam hypotension is managed by stopping infusion and fluids.

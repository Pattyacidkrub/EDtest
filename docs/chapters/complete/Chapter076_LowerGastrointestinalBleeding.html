Gastrointestinal Disorders

Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Lower GI bleeding is bleeding distal to the ligament of Treitz, but the ED mindset starts broader: stabilize first, then decide whether hematochezia is truly lower GI, brisk upper GI, anorectal, gynecologic, urinary, medication-colored stool, or vascular catastrophe.

Board trap: Hematochezia plus hemodynamic instability can be brisk upper GI bleeding, not just colon bleeding.

LOWER GASTROINTESTINAL BLEEDING

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

Lower GI bleeding is common and can be life-threatening until proven otherwise. Many episodes stop spontaneously, but early course is unpredictable and the bleeding source is often not found on the first encounter. Among confirmed lower-GI sources, diverticular disease is most common, followed by colitis, hemorrhoids, and neoplasia/polyps.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Hematochezia is bright red or maroon rectal bleeding. Melena usually reflects an upper source but can occur with slow transit from a lower source. Stool color is a clue, not a localization test.

DIVERTICULOSIS

Diverticular bleeding is classically painless and arterial from erosion into a penetrating vessel. It can be massive, often stops spontaneously, and can recur. Elderly patients, anticoagulant/NSAID users, and patients with comorbid disease have higher risk.

VASCULAR ECTASIA

Vascular ectasia includes angiodysplasia and AVMs, usually from degenerative vascular change with aging. It can bleed intermittently and may be hard to identify without colonoscopy, CTA, angiography, or other localization.

ISCHEMIC COLITIS AND MESENTERIC ISCHEMIA

Ischemic colitis often causes crampy abdominal pain and bloody diarrhea in older or vascular-risk patients and may resolve or progress. Mesenteric ischemia is less common but far more dangerous; abdominal pain out of proportion, atrial fibrillation, vascular disease, or lactate elevation should escalate concern. Delayed mesenteric ischemia diagnosis carries high mortality.

MECKEL'S DIVERTICULUM

Meckel's diverticulum is rare but important, especially in younger patients. Ectopic gastric mucosa can ulcerate adjacent ileal mucosa and cause bleeding.

HEMORRHOIDS

Hemorrhoids are a common anorectal source, usually painless and associated with bowel movements. Massive hemorrhage is unusual, so persistent instability should trigger a search for another source.

OTHER CAUSES OF LOWER GI BLEEDING

Other causes include infectious colitis, inflammatory bowel disease, radiation colitis, rectal ulcers, trauma, foreign bodies, delayed postpolypectomy bleeding, malignancy, endometriosis, Dieulafoy lesions, colonic varices, portal hypertensive enteropathy, LVAD-related bleeding, and aortoenteric fistula. Prior aortic graft plus GI bleeding is a surgical emergency warning.

DIAGNOSIS

History, exam, diagnostics, and resuscitation often happen simultaneously. High-morbidity signals include instability, repeated hematochezia, gross blood on rectal exam, syncope, initial hematocrit under 35%, elevated creatinine, aspirin/NSAID use, diverticulosis/angioectasia history, and multiple comorbidities.

HISTORY

Ask about hematochezia, melena, syncope, weakness, angina, altered mental status, prior GI bleeding, colonoscopy or polypectomy, trauma, inserted/ingested foreign bodies, weight loss, bowel habit change, aortic graft, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, anticoagulants, iron, bismuth, and foods such as beets. Not every red or black stool is blood, but do not use that possibility to dismiss shock.

PHYSICAL EXAMINATION

Assess shock, perfusion, pulse pressure, capillary refill, abdominal tenderness/mass/ascites, liver disease signs, petechiae/purpura, anorectal trauma, fissure, external hemorrhoids, vaginal or urinary mimics, and gross blood. Digital rectal exam and anoscopy may identify distal sources, but blood above the anoscope means the search is not finished.

LABORATORY TESTING

Obtain CBC, coagulation studies, platelets, type and crossmatch, BUN, creatinine, and electrolytes. Initial hematocrit may lag behind actual blood loss. Elevated BUN:creatinine can suggest an upper source from digested blood but cannot exclude lower bleeding.

IMAGING

Plain films and routine chest radiographs have limited value unless obstruction, perforation, foreign body, or pulmonary disease is suspected. Barium studies interfere with urgent endoscopy/angiography. CTA can localize active/recent bleeding rapidly and guide interventional radiology; tagged RBC scan can localize intermittent bleeding but is slower and not therapeutic.

TREATMENT

Resuscitate unstable or actively bleeding patients. Give oxygen if hypoxic, monitor cardiac rhythm, place two large-bore IVs, begin crystalloid and blood product support as indicated, and correct coagulopathy when INR is elevated or platelets are below 50,000/µL. Transfuse for clinical significant blood loss or ongoing bleeding; do not rely on the first hematocrit alone.

Consider an NG tube only when significant hematochezia raises concern for an upper source and the result will change immediate intervention. NG aspiration has low negative predictive value, so a negative aspirate cannot rule out UGIB. Obtain early GI, IR, and surgical consultation for severe or ongoing bleeding.

ENDOSCOPY AND SURGERY

Flexible sigmoidoscopy evaluates distal sources but misses proximal bleeding. Colonoscopy can diagnose diverticulosis, angiodysplasia, colitis, malignancy, and postpolypectomy bleeding and can provide hemostasis with injection, coagulation, clips, banding, or other methods. If colonoscopy fails or bleeding is brisk/unstable, CTA/angiography, embolization, upper endoscopy, or surgery may be needed.

DISPOSITION AND FOLLOW-UP

Most lower GI hemorrhage patients require hospital admission and early endoscopist involvement. ICU is appropriate for active bleeding or instability. Outpatient care is reserved for obvious mild anorectal bleeding, hemodynamic stability, no maroon/melanotic stool or concerning rectal blood, no major comorbidities, and reliable follow-up. There is no universally reliable ED discharge score for lower GI bleeding comparable to upper-GI scores.

Pulmonary Disorders

Lung Empyema and Abscess

Chapter 66 is a source-control chapter: empyema is infected pleural space and lung abscess is necrotic infected lung parenchyma. Both can masquerade as slow pneumonia failure, so the ED decision is to recognize complicated infection, image intelligently, start appropriate antibiotics, and arrange drainage or admission.

Board trap: persistent pneumonia symptoms despite therapy are not just antibiotic failure; think parapneumonic effusion, empyema, abscess, obstruction, TB, malignancy, or an unusual cavitary mimic.

LUNG EMPYEMA AND ABSCESS

Empyema and abscess overlap clinically but live in different compartments. Empyema is pleural pus requiring pleural source control; abscess is lung parenchymal necrosis that often drains through the bronchial tree but may need invasive drainage if complicated.

EMPYEMA

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

Empyema is pus in the pleural space. A parapneumonic effusion is a pleural effusion associated with lung infection, most often pneumonia and less often lung abscess. Bacterial pneumonia with parapneumonic effusion is the classic precursor. Do not wait for dramatic toxicity; empyema may evolve insidiously after what initially looked like routine pneumonia.

Other causes include chest surgery, trauma, esophageal perforation, chest tube or thoracentesis complications, extension from subdiaphragmatic infection, and infected hemothorax, chylothorax, or hydrothorax. Predisposing factors include aspiration, impaired ciliary clearance, immunocompromise, malignancy, IV drug use, alcohol use disorder, diabetes, reflux, and poor oral hygiene.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Suspect empyema when fever, cough, dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, or malaise persists despite pneumonia treatment. Patients may appear chronically ill with weight loss, anemia, or night sweats. Exam clues are pleural-space clues: decreased breath sounds, dullness to percussion, decreased tactile fremitus, splinting, and sometimes friction rub.

DIAGNOSIS

Diagnostic criteria include aspiration of grossly purulent material or pleural fluid with positive Gram stain or culture, glucose less than 40 mg/dL, pH less than 7.2, or LDH greater than 1000 IU/L. Pleural fluid pH below 7.2 is a source-control warning, not a trivia value. In tuberculosis-associated empyema, pleural lymphocytes and biopsy/culture may be needed.

Chest radiography can show a pleural-based opacity, but ultrasound better differentiates effusion from empyema and can guide thoracentesis or tube placement. CT with IV contrast assesses loculation, mass, abscess, and alternative pathology.

TREATMENT

The definitive treatment is drainage plus antibiotics. Antibiotics without source control are not adequate for established empyema. Therapy also treats the underlying disease, especially pneumonia, and symptomatic care avoids NSAIDs or opioids that can blunt pleuritic pain and decrease pleural drainage.

Antibiotic selection depends on presentation, local patterns, cultures, and likely source. Community infection commonly needs anaerobic and gram-positive coverage; postprocedure or hospital-acquired empyema should prompt MRSA and Pseudomonas coverage. Monotherapy with penicillin derivative or metronidazole alone is inadequate because mixed flora and Staphylococcus coverage matter.

Fibrinolytics may improve drainage of loculated pleural infection. Alteplase plus DNase has evidence for better drainage, fewer surgical referrals, and shorter stay in selected patients, whereas either agent alone is not reliably effective. Patients who fail antibiotics and tube drainage may require video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or thoracotomy.

LUNG ABSCESS

INTRODUCTION AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Lung abscess is localized necrosis of lung parenchyma, usually from suppurative infection after aspiration of oral contents. Common organisms include anaerobes and streptococci, with S. aureus, Klebsiella, tuberculosis, Actinomyces, fungi, and parasites depending on host and exposure. Primary abscess is often aspiration-driven; secondary abscess should make you look for malignancy, immunosuppression, sepsis, extrapulmonary infection, or surgical complication.

Abscesses may also arise from hematogenous spread, septic emboli from endocarditis, or infarcted lung. Acute abscess lasts less than 1 month; chronic abscess lasts more than 1 month and broadens the differential.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Lung abscess usually has an indolent 2-to-4-week course with cough, fever, pleuritic chest pain, hemoptysis, weight loss, and night sweats. Fever, tachycardia, and tachypnea can be absent because the infection develops slowly. A foul or productive cough helps, but absence of sputum does not exclude abscess.

DIAGNOSIS

Chest radiograph may show dense consolidation or an air-fluid level inside a cavity, suggesting communication with the bronchial tree. Chest CT better identifies cavitary lesions, bronchopleural fistula, obstruction, malignancy, and foreign body aspiration. A cavitary lesion is a differential diagnosis, not a diagnosis of simple bacterial abscess.

TREATMENT

After stabilization, empiric antibiotics target anaerobes and facultatively anaerobic streptococci. Ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV every 6 hours or a carbapenem is appropriate; clindamycin is an option but often avoided because of C. difficile risk. Drainage usually occurs spontaneously through the tracheobronchial tree and is suggested by an air-fluid level.

Failure to respond to antibiotics should trigger evaluation for percutaneous, transbronchial, or open drainage. CT- or ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage can provide culture material and source control, but complications and contamination risk must be considered.

DISPOSITION

Admit newly diagnosed lung abscess for IV antibiotics and evaluation for complications. Surgical or percutaneous intervention is reserved for large, complicated abscesses or antibiotic failure. Reasons for failure include bronchial obstruction, nonbacterial causes, large cavity greater than 6 cm, empyema, and mycobacteria.

1. General Approach to Bites & Stings

Tintinalli Ch.211 p.1351
  • Most bites/stings cause minor local reactions (pain, swelling, itching).
  • The two life-threatening pathways are (1) anaphylaxis from an immune response and (2) systemic envenomation from venom toxicity (neurotoxic, hemotoxic, or cardiotoxic).
  • Workup focuses on identifying the species when possible, distinguishing local vs systemic effects, and excluding secondary infection.
  • Tetanus prophylaxis per immunization status, wound cleansing, and pain control are universal first steps.

General principles

  1. Remove the stinger if present (bee, hornet) — flick or scrape; do not squeeze residual venom sac.
  2. Wound cleansing: soap and water; antiseptic.
  3. Cool compresses, elevation, NSAIDs for pain.
  4. Watch for anaphylaxis — observe ≥30–60 min for severe reactions.
  5. Tetanus prophylaxis: Tdap if not up to date; TIG for high-risk wound + incomplete primary series.
  6. Photograph or capture the offending creature when safe — aids identification and antivenom selection.

2. Anaphylaxis from Insect Stings

Tintinalli p.1351
  • Symptoms emerge along a continuous spectrum starting within 15 minutes (most commonly within minutes) and may progress over hours.
  • Cutaneous findings (urticaria, flushing, pruritus, angioedema) are the most common — but absent in up to 10–20%, so absence does not rule out anaphylaxis.
  • Respiratory: stridor, hoarseness, wheezing, dyspnea, hypoxemia.
  • GI: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea (often early in sting-related anaphylaxis).
  • Cardiovascular: tachycardia, hypotension, syncope, dysrhythmia, shock.
  • Neurologic: anxiety, "feeling of impending doom," seizures.

Treatment

  1. IM Epinephrine 0.3–0.5 mg (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) into the lateral thigh — first-line, repeat every 5–15 minutes if no response. NEVER give SQ for severe reactions; IM has faster, more reliable absorption.
  2. Oxygen, IV access, fluid bolus 1–2 L NS if hypotensive.
  3. Inhaled β2-agonist for bronchospasm.
  4. Adjuncts (do NOT replace epinephrine): antihistamines (H1 + H2), corticosteroids — these address rash and biphasic reaction risk, not the airway/cardiovascular emergency.
  5. Glucagon 1 mg IV if patient on β-blockers and refractory hypotension.
  6. Observe at least 4–6 h after symptom resolution to monitor for biphasic reactions.
  7. Refractory shock: epinephrine infusion 0.1–1 µg/kg/min; vasopressin if persistent hypotension.
⚠️ Pearl — Refractory anaphylaxis on β-blockers: β-blockade blunts epinephrine's effect. Glucagon 1 mg IV bolus then 1–5 mg/h infusion bypasses β-receptors and is recommended.

Long-term care

  • Two epinephrine auto-injectors at discharge — patient and caregiver education.
  • Allergist referral for venom immunotherapy (VIT) — reduces future anaphylaxis risk by ~98% in confirmed cases.
  • Avoidance counseling — clothing, footwear, food handling outdoors.

3. Hymenoptera — Bees, Wasps, Hornets, Ants

Tintinalli p.1351

Members

  • Apidae: honey bees, bumblebees — barbed stinger remains in skin (only honey bees).
  • Vespidae: yellow jackets, hornets, wasps — smooth stinger, can sting repeatedly.
  • Formicidae: fire ants (Solenopsis), harvester ants — produce sterile pustules.

Local reaction

  • Localized erythema, edema, pruritus, pain. Resolves in hours to days.
  • Cool compresses, NSAIDs, antihistamines, topical steroid for severe pruritus.
  • Stinger removal: scrape with credit card or fingernail; squeezing the embedded sac may inject more venom.

Systemic toxic reaction

  • Massive envenomation (≥50 stings in adult; ≥10 in child) → rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, AKI, hepatic injury, hypertension, DIC.
  • Treatment: aggressive IV crystalloid + alkalinization + electrolyte management.

Fire ants (Solenopsis)

  • Bite then sting in a circular pattern → grouped sterile pustules.
  • Local treatment; pustules should NOT be unroofed (sterile until manipulated).
  • Anaphylaxis can occur on first or subsequent exposures.
Massive envenomation pearl: ≥50 stings in adult or ≥10 in child can cause systemic toxicity even WITHOUT allergy. Hospitalize and monitor 24 h for rhabdomyolysis, AKI, and DIC.

5. Brown Recluse (Loxosceles reclusa)

Tintinalli p.1352–1353

Identification & Distribution

  • Violin/fiddle-shaped marking on the cephalothorax — the characteristic feature.
  • Eyes: three pairs (six eyes) in a U-shape — distinguishes from most spiders (which have eight eyes).
  • Body 1–2 cm with leg span 2–3 cm.
  • Highest US density in central southern states; sequester in dark dry areas (closets, basements, woodpiles).
📚 Fig 211-1 — Highest density range of recluse spiders in the US (Tintinalli p.1352)
📚 Fig 211-2 — Brown recluse with violin-shaped marking and three pairs of eyes (Tintinalli p.1353)

Pathophysiology

  • Venom contains sphingomyelinase D + multiple proteases → endothelial damage, platelet aggregation, thrombosis, complement activation.
  • Most bites cause minor local reactions; ~10% develop necrotic ulcer (cutaneous loxoscelism).
  • Rarely systemic (viscerocutaneous loxoscelism) — hemolysis, DIC, AKI; more common in children.

Clinical features

  • Initially painless — bite often unrecognized.
  • Within 1–2 hours: pain, erythema, central pallor.
  • By 24–48 h: classic "red, white, and blue" sign (central erythema → blanched ischemia → cyanotic eschar).
  • Necrotic ulcer evolves over 1–4 weeks; healing leaves scar.
  • Systemic features (within 24–72 h): fever, chills, malaise, vomiting, hemoglobinuria, AKI, DIC.
📚 Fig 211-3 — Early brown recluse bite (~8 h) with central erythema and violaceous spread (Tintinalli p.1353)

Treatment

  • Supportive — wound care, cool compress, elevation, NSAIDs, tetanus prophylaxis.
  • Antibiotics — only if secondary infection; do not give prophylactically.
  • Dapsone — historically used to limit necrosis; evidence weak; risk of methemoglobinemia and hemolysis (esp. G6PD-deficient patients).
  • Surgical debridement only after wound demarcates (typically 2–6 weeks); early debridement worsens outcomes.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen, steroids, antivenom — not validated in US; investigational.
  • Monitor for systemic toxicity: CBC, BUN/Cr, urinalysis (hemoglobinuria), serial.
Pearl — "spider bite" mimics: patients often present claiming a spider bite; usually it's MRSA furuncle. Real spider bites are rarely witnessed; if no spider was seen and lesion looks like an abscess — treat as MRSA, not as bite.

6. Black Widow (Latrodectus)

Tintinalli p.1353–1354

Identification

  • Adult female: glossy black, 1–1.5 cm body with characteristic red hourglass on ventral abdomen.
  • Males and juveniles are non-toxic.
  • Distribution: worldwide; in US, southwestern and southern states.
📚 Fig 211-4 — Female black widow with red hourglass marking and offspring (Tintinalli p.1353)

Pathophysiology

  • Venom contains α-latrotoxin → calcium-dependent neurotransmitter release → autonomic stimulation and severe muscle cramping.
  • Pain peaks at 1–3 hours; symptoms persist 24–72 h.

Clinical features (latrodectism)

  • Bite: pinprick pain (mild), faint local lesion 1–2 cm, often blanched halo with surrounding erythema.
  • Within 30–60 min: severe muscle cramps spreading from extremity to trunk and abdomen.
  • Abdominal rigidity — can mimic acute abdomen; "black widow rigidity".
  • Sympathomimetic features: hypertension, tachycardia, diaphoresis, mydriasis.
  • Other: nausea/vomiting, headache, paresthesias, ptosis, periorbital edema (facies latrodectismica).
  • Severe: respiratory depression, seizures, hemodynamic compromise — pediatric, elderly, comorbidities at higher risk.
📚 Fig 211-5 — Black widow spider bite (clinical photo): central pinprick lesion with surrounding erythema (Tintinalli p.1354)

Treatment

  • Pain control: opioids (morphine, fentanyl) titrated; benzodiazepines for muscle spasms.
  • IV calcium gluconate — historically used; modern evidence shows limited benefit and is no longer first-line.
  • Latrodectus antivenin (Merck, US) — for severe envenomation:
    • Indications: severe pain refractory to opioids/benzos, hemodynamic instability, pregnant patients, age <5 or >65, respiratory distress.
    • Dose: 1 vial IV diluted in 50 mL NS over 15–30 min; can repeat in 1 h.
    • Risk: anaphylaxis (equine source); pretreat with antihistamines; have epinephrine ready.
    • Rapid relief in 30 min if effective.
  • Tetanus prophylaxis per immunization status.
  • Disposition: symptomatic patients → admit for ≥24 h observation.

Answer: B. Latrodectism — diffuse muscle cramping, abdominal rigidity, sympathomimetic features. Reference: Tintinalli p.1354.

7. Australian Funnel-Web Spider (Atrax robustus)

Tintinalli p.1355
  • Most lethal spider in the world; native to eastern Australia.
  • Females larger than males but males are more dangerous.
  • Bite causes severe pain at site, then rapid systemic envenomation.

Clinical features

  • Local: intense pain at bite, sweating, piloerection, pulmonary congestion.
  • Systemic (within 30 min – 2 h): hypertension, tachycardia, agitation, lacrimation, salivation, paresthesias, fasciculations, muscle spasms, vomiting, abdominal pain, pulmonary edema, cardiac arrest.
  • Death from massive sympathetic discharge with cardiogenic shock or pulmonary edema, typically 15–90 min after envenomation.

Treatment

  • Immediate pressure-immobilization bandage — wide elastic bandage from bite site over the entire limb at firm pressure (similar to snakebite first aid in Australia); reduces venom dissemination.
  • Splint the limb in functional position; immobilization.
  • Atrax antivenom — life-saving; available in Australia. Treat symptomatic patients early; do not wait.
  • Supportive: airway management, hemodynamic support.
  • Hospital monitoring at least 24 h after antivenom — risk of recurrent symptoms.
⚠️ Pearl: Funnel-web spider envenomation can kill within 15–90 minutes. Pressure-immobilization first aid + emergency transport + antivenom is the standard. NSAIDs/opioids alone do not stop progression.

8. Other Spiders

Tintinalli p.1354–1355

Phoneutria (armed / banana spider)

  • South American — extremely painful bite + autonomic chaos (both sympathetic AND parasympathetic features simultaneously).
  • Severe priapism, miosis or mydriasis, salivation, sweating, hypertension or hypotension, dysrhythmia.
  • Antivenom available in Brazil; otherwise supportive.

Tarantula

  • Bite painful but rarely produces systemic envenomation.
  • Urticating hairs released into the air can cause severe ocular and respiratory irritation; ophthalmology consultation if eye exposure.
  • Tear with adhesive tape to remove embedded hairs from skin.

Hobo spider (Tegenaria agrestis / Eratigena agrestis)

  • Found in Pacific Northwest US.
  • Earlier reports of necrotic arachnidism are now not supported by recent evidence.
  • Treat with supportive care; necrotic ulcers should prompt search for alternative cause.

Yellow sac, wolf, jumping, daddy long-legs spiders

  • Generally minor local reactions only.
  • Daddy long-legs (Pholcidae) — commonly thought lethal; they are NOT (myth).

9. Scorpions (Scorpionidae)

Tintinalli Table 211-2 p.1356
  • Worldwide distribution; few species cause significant systemic toxicity.
  • In US: Centruroides sculpturatus (bark scorpion) — found in Arizona, New Mexico, southern California, parts of Texas. Capable of life-threatening envenomation in children.
  • Other dangerous species: Tityus (Brazil), Androctonus, Buthus, Mesobuthus, Leiurus.

Pathophysiology

  • Venom contains sodium and potassium channel-blocking neurotoxins → repetitive firing of sympathetic and parasympathetic neurons.
  • Effects: cholinergic and sympathetic crisis simultaneously.

Clinical features

  • Local: immediate burning pain at sting site; minimal swelling/redness.
  • Systemic — Centruroides bark scorpion (especially in children <10):
    • Restlessness, agitation, opisthotonus
    • Roving eye movements ("oculomotor crisis"; pathognomonic)
    • Cranial nerve dysfunction — slurred speech, tongue fasciculations, dysphagia
    • Muscle fasciculations, twitching, jerking
    • Hypersalivation, lacrimation, sweating
    • Tachycardia, hypertension, pulmonary edema
    • Severe: respiratory failure, cardiac arrest
📚 Table 211-2 — Scorpion Sting Effects, Pathophysiology, and Treatment (Tintinalli p.1356)

Treatment

  • Local: NSAIDs, ice, lidocaine without epinephrine, tetanus prophylaxis.
  • Systemic envenomation (especially pediatric Centruroides):
    • Anascorp (Centruroides immune F(ab')2) — FDA-approved antivenom for severe Centruroides; rapid resolution within 60 min.
    • Benzodiazepines for agitation, fasciculations.
    • Atropine for cholinergic excess (only if symptomatic).
    • Prazosin for sympathetic-driven HTN/cardiomyopathy (Indian/African experience with Mesobuthus).
    • Avoid opioids — may worsen respiratory depression; minimize sedation.
    • Supportive: airway management, hemodynamic support.

10. Chiggers, Mosquitoes, Fleas, Lice — Vector and Nuisance Bites

Tintinalli p.1356

Chiggers (Trombiculidae)

  • Larvae attach to skin around tight clothing (waist, groin, ankles); produce localized pruritus and erythema after enzymatic digestion of host tissue.
  • Develop intensely pruritic erythematous papules ("chigger bites").
  • Symptomatic treatment — topical/oral antihistamines, topical steroids; secondary infection treated with antibiotics if present.
  • Vector for scrub typhus in Asia (Orientia tsutsugamushi) — eschar at bite site + fever + lymphadenopathy.

Mosquitoes

  • Local pruritic reaction; severe allergic reactions possible.
  • Vector for many diseases: malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Zika, yellow fever, West Nile, Eastern Equine Encephalitis, Japanese encephalitis, lymphatic filariasis.
  • Treatment: antihistamines, topical steroids; treat any vector-borne illness specifically.
  • Prevention: DEET, picaridin, permethrin-treated clothing, mosquito nets.

Fleas

  • Cat (Ctenocephalides felis) and dog flea (most common); also human flea, rat flea.
  • Linear papular eruption at exposed skin (ankles, calves).
  • Vector for: plague (Yersinia pestis via rat flea), murine typhus, cat-scratch disease, bartonellosis.
  • Treatment: antihistamines, topical steroids; treat secondary infection.

Lice

  • Head lice (Pediculus humanus capitis), body lice, pubic lice (Phthirus pubis).
  • Diagnosis: visual identification of nits (eggs) attached to hair shafts; live lice on inspection.
  • Body lice vector: epidemic typhus (Rickettsia prowazekii), trench fever, relapsing fever.
  • Treatment:
    • Permethrin 1% rinse — first-line for head lice; apply for 10 min; repeat in 7–10 days.
    • Malathion 0.5% for resistant cases.
    • Ivermectin 200 µg/kg PO single dose if topical fails.
    • Wash bedding, clothing in hot water; vacuum carpets/furniture.
    • Treat household contacts.
Vector-borne disease pearl: always ask about geographic exposure with a febrile patient and arthropod bite history. Eschar at bite site + fever + headache + lymphadenopathy = consider rickettsial disease (scrub typhus, Mediterranean spotted fever). Empirical doxycycline can be life-saving while awaiting confirmation.

⚕️ ATLS 11th — Envenomation in Trauma Settings

ATLS
  • Trauma patients in outdoor environments are at risk for combined mechanical injury and envenomation.
  • Anaphylaxis can mimic hemorrhagic shock initially — both cause hypotension and tachycardia. Distinguishing features: rash/wheezing/known sting suggests anaphylaxis; obvious bleeding or trauma mechanism suggests hemorrhage. Treat both: epinephrine + volume resuscitation + tourniquet/hemostasis.
  • Pressure-immobilization first aid — appropriate for funnel-web spider, snakebite, some scorpion stings (depending on regional guideline). NOT for hymenoptera, brown recluse, black widow.
  • Avoid the "cut and suck" method, ice, tourniquets in most spider/snake/scorpion envenomations — increases tissue damage.
  • Wound management: tetanus prophylaxis, antibiotic coverage if devitalized tissue/contaminated wound.
  • Compartment syndrome can develop after envenomation with significant local edema (especially funnel-web, severe widow); monitor.

📗 Rosen's EM 10th — Bites and Stings

Rosen's Ch.55 (Arthropod Bites and Stings)
  • Same overall framework with greater detail on regional / international variation.
  • Anaphylaxis treatment aligns with WAO/EAACI guidelines: IM epinephrine first, antihistamines and steroids strictly adjuncts.
  • Brown recluse loxoscelism: Rosen's emphasizes that systemic ("viscerocutaneous") loxoscelism is far more common in children and may present with severe hemolysis/AKI/DIC — admit pediatric Loxosceles bites with systemic symptoms.
  • Latrodectus antivenin: Rosen's lists more cautious indications — reserved for severe envenomation due to anaphylaxis and serum sickness risk.
  • Centruroides scorpion sting: recommends Anascorp early in symptomatic pediatric patients; describes rapid resolution within 60 min.
  • Pressure immobilization bandage is recommended for Australian funnel-web spider but NOT for North American spiders, snakes, or scorpions — restricts venom but may worsen tissue damage with cytotoxic venoms.
  • Tick paralysis (covered in Ch.56 of Rosen's) — ascending flaccid paralysis; treatment is tick removal; rapid recovery.

🔑 Key Points (High-Yield)

  1. Anaphylaxis from any bite/sting → IM epinephrine 0.3–0.5 mg lateral thigh first-line; antihistamines, steroids, bronchodilators are adjuncts only.
  2. β-blocker patient + refractory anaphylaxis → glucagon 1 mg IV bolus.
  3. Massive Hymenoptera envenomation (≥50 stings adult / ≥10 child) → systemic toxicity (rhabdo, AKI, hemolysis); hospitalize.
  4. Brown recluse (Loxosceles) — violin marking + 6 eyes; "red, white, blue" sign at 24–48 h; supportive care; debride only after demarcation.
  5. Loxosceles in children → watch for systemic toxicity (hemolysis, AKI, DIC).
  6. Black widow (Latrodectus) — α-latrotoxin → severe cramps + abdominal rigidity + sympathomimetic crisis; first-line opioids + benzos.
  7. Latrodectus antivenin reserved for severe envenomation, pregnancy, refractory pain (anaphylaxis risk).
  8. Australian funnel-web (Atrax) — most lethal; pressure-immobilization first aid + antivenom; can kill in 15–90 min.
  9. Centruroides bark scorpion in children — roving eye movements + cranial nerve findings + cholinergic excess; treat with Anascorp.
  10. "Spider bite" in the ED is usually MRSA — question the diagnosis if unwitnessed.
  11. Pressure-immobilization appropriate for funnel-web + Australian snakebite; NOT for North American spiders/scorpions.
  12. Vector-borne disease — fever + eschar + arthropod bite → consider rickettsial; empiric doxycycline can be life-saving.
  13. Head lice → permethrin 1% topical first-line; ivermectin oral for resistant.

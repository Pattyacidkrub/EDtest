Gastrointestinal Disorders

Diverticulitis

Diverticulitis in the ED is a severity and disposition problem: decide whether the patient is uncomplicated and safe for outpatient care or has complicated disease, sepsis risk, abscess, perforation, obstruction, fistula, immunocompromise, or poor follow-up.

Board trap: Do not treat every LLQ pain as simple outpatient diverticulitis; colon cancer, appendicitis, IBD, ischemia, GU/GYN disease, and complicated diverticulitis stay in the differential.

DIVERTICULITIS

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

Diverticular disease increases with age and is common in industrialized nations. Most diverticulosis never becomes diverticulitis; many uncomplicated episodes can be managed medically, while complicated disease drives morbidity and admission.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Diverticula are herniations where the vasa recta penetrate the circular muscle layer. Inflammation begins with microperforation and bacterial translocation, and may progress to abscess, phlegmon, fistula, obstruction, or free perforation. NSAIDs, opioids, and steroids increase perforation risk.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Classic disease causes LLQ pain, fever, and leukocytosis. Redundant sigmoid or right-sided disease may cause suprapubic or RLQ pain. Nausea, vomiting, anorexia, urinary symptoms, mild distention, hypoactive bowel sounds, guarding, rebound, sterile pyuria, or a tender mass suggest extent and complications.

DIAGNOSIS

Stable patients with a prior confirmed episode and similar symptoms may not always need immediate diagnostic evaluation, but first episodes, atypical features, worsening symptoms, or concern for complications require imaging and broader workup. Labs are rarely diagnostic but help assess severity, renal function, leukocytosis, CRP, alternatives, and medication safety.

IMAGING

CT abdomen/pelvis is preferred because it confirms diverticulitis, excludes mimics, and detects complications. Findings include pericolic fat inflammation, diverticula, bowel wall thickening, phlegmon, abscess, obstruction, fistula, free air, or fluid. Ultrasound is operator dependent and limited for complications. CT risk factors for progression include fluid collections, a long inflamed colon segment, and inflamed diverticulum >2 cm.

TREATMENT

Treatment depends on uncomplicated versus complicated disease. Uncomplicated disease is inflammation confined to bowel wall or a small localized abscess; complicated disease involves abscess, obstruction, fistula, or perforation. Antibiotics historically anchored care, but selective nonantibiotic management can be appropriate for CT-confirmed mild uncomplicated disease in immunocompetent patients without systemic infection signs.

DISPOSITION AND FOLLOW-UP

Disposition is the chapter’s operational endpoint. Outpatient management is reasonable only for uncomplicated disease with normal vital signs, mild to moderate tenderness, no distention/vomiting, PO tolerance, pain control, follow-up in 2 to 3 days, and ability to self-care. Admit complicated diverticulitis and high-risk patients. High-risk factors include age >70, fever, vomiting/inability to tolerate PO, poor follow-up, multiple comorbidities, immunocompromise, steroid use, malnutrition, active malignancy, chronic opioid use, generalized pain, high WBC/CRP, sepsis signs, and CT risk factors.

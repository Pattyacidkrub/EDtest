1. Introduction & Epidemiology

Tintinalli Ch.210 p.1345
  • Heat-related illnesses span a continuum from minor cramps to life-threatening heat stroke.
  • Mortality from heat exposure varies widely; heat-wave events can produce thousands of excess deaths (e.g., European heat wave 2003 — >14,800 deaths in France).
  • Vulnerable groups: elderly, very young, athletes, outdoor laborers, military, those with chronic illness, on anticholinergic/sympathomimetic/antipsychotic medications, alcohol use disorder, and inadequate housing/access to air conditioning.
  • Two main heat-stroke patterns:
    • Classic (non-exertional) — elderly, debilitated, during heat waves; gradual onset; often anhidrotic.
    • Exertional — young, fit individuals exercising in heat; rapid onset; often sweating preserved.

2. Pathophysiology

Tintinalli p.1345–1346

Heat balance equation

Body temperature = heat produced (metabolism, exercise) − heat dissipated (radiation, conduction, convection, evaporation).

Mechanisms of heat transfer

MechanismDescriptionEffective when
RadiationInfrared energy emitted to cooler surroundings (~65% at rest)Ambient temp < skin temp
ConductionDirect contact transfer (skin to cool surface or air)Low ambient (cold immersion)
ConvectionMovement of air/water across skin (~15%)Wind, fans
EvaporationSweat → vapor (~25–30% at rest, dominant when ambient ≥35°C)Low humidity
Critical point: when ambient temperature exceeds skin temperature (~35°C / 95°F), radiation, conduction, and convection lose efficacy — the body becomes dependent on evaporation. High humidity (>75% relative humidity) blocks evaporation → rapid heat accumulation and risk of heat stroke.

Response to heat stress

  • Cutaneous vasodilation → ↑ skin blood flow up to 8 L/min → heat loss to environment.
  • Sweating — onset within minutes; peak rate 1.5–2 L/h; obligate Na⁺ and water loss.
  • Tachycardia and ↑ cardiac output to maintain BP despite peripheral vasodilation.
  • Acclimatization — over 7–14 days of repeated exposure: ↑ sweat volume, ↓ sweat sodium concentration (lower salt loss), ↑ plasma volume, ↓ HR for given workload.
Inline MCQ 1. When ambient temperature exceeds 35°C (95°F), which heat dissipation mechanism becomes the body's primary defense?

B. Conduction
C. Convection
D. Evaporation
Answer: D. Once ambient temp exceeds skin temp, radiative/conductive/convective losses fail; evaporation becomes the dominant cooling mechanism — and is impaired by high humidity. Reference: Tintinalli p.1345.

4. Prickly Heat (Miliaria Rubra)

Tintinalli p.1347
  • Pruritic, papular, erythematous rash on heat-exposed skin areas.
  • Caused by sweat duct blockage in stratum corneum, with sweat retention → inflammation.
  • Typically affects covered/macerated regions (back, chest, axillae).
  • Treatment: cool environment, dry skin, light loose clothing, avoid antiperspirants. Topical chlorhexidine prevents secondary S. aureus infection. Salicylic-acid lotion can desquamate plugged ducts.

5. Heat Cramps

Tintinalli p.1347
  • Painful, involuntary, spasmodic contractions of skeletal muscle (calves, thighs, shoulders) during/after vigorous exertion in heat.
  • Mechanism: relative hyponatremia + hypochloremia from sweat replacement with hypotonic fluids (water alone).
  • Core temperature is normal; mental status is normal.
  • Treatment:
    • Stop exertion, rest in cool area.
    • Oral electrolyte solution (~0.1–0.2% NaCl) — e.g., 1 tsp salt in 500 mL water, or sports drinks.
    • IV NS only if severe / can't tolerate PO.
    • Salt tablets are not recommended — gastric irritation, may cause nausea.
  • Prevention: pre-hydrate, scheduled fluid breaks during exertion, electrolyte replacement.
Inline MCQ 2. Heat cramps result primarily from:
A. Pure dehydration
B. Lactic acidosis
C. Hyponatremia/hypochloremia from replacing sweat with hypotonic fluid
D. Hypocalcemia
Answer: C. Sodium loss in sweat replaced with water → relative hyponatremia/hypochloremia → muscle spasms. Reference: Tintinalli p.1347.

6. Heat Exhaustion (Heat Stress)

Tintinalli p.1347
  • Result of prolonged heat exposure with water and/or salt depletion. Two patterns:
    • Water-depleted (hypernatremic): inadequate fluid replacement; thirst, weakness, hyperthermia.
    • Salt-depleted (hyponatremic): inadequate sodium replacement (water-only); profound weakness, cramps, nausea/vomiting.
  • Core temperature <40°C; sweating profuse; mental status mostly normal (may be mildly impaired).
  • Symptoms: headache, weakness, nausea/vomiting, dizziness, orthostatic hypotension, tachycardia, syncope.
  • Lab features: ↑ Hct (hemoconcentration), variable Na, hyperthermia <40°C, mildly elevated CK, ABG often normal.
  • Treatment:
    • Move to cool environment.
    • Volume + electrolyte replacement (oral if tolerating; IV NS 1–2 L if not).
    • Rapid rehydration of severely dehydrated patients with isotonic crystalloid.
    • Cooling not typically needed unless borderline temperature; passive measures suffice.
Pearl: the line between heat exhaustion and heat stroke is altered mental status + temp >40°C. If either is present, treat as heat stroke immediately.

7. Heat Stroke — Life-Threatening Emergency

Tintinalli p.1347–1348

Cardinal features

  • Hyperthermia >40°C (104°F)
  • Altered mental status — delirium, ataxia, agitation, seizures, coma
  • Setting consistent with heat exposure

Two clinical patterns

FeatureClassic (non-exertional)Exertional
PopulationElderly, debilitated, chronic illnessYoung, fit, athletes / soldiers / laborers
OnsetDays during heat waveHours during exertion
SweatingOften anhidroticOften preserved sweating
SettingIndoor, no air conditioningOutdoor exertion
RhabdomyolysisLess commonCommon (severe)
Renal failureLess commonCommon (myoglobinuric ATN)
DIC / hepatic failureCommon late findingCommon late finding
⚠️ Mortality is directly related to magnitude and duration of hyperthermia. Heat stroke is a true emergency — every minute of delay in cooling raises mortality. Target: cool to <39°C (102°F) within 30 minutes.

CNS manifestations (almost universal)

  • Confusion, disorientation, agitation, irritability
  • Cerebellar findings — ataxia, dysarthria
  • Seizures (focal or generalized)
  • Decorticate or decerebrate posturing
  • Coma

Answer: C. Temp >40°C + altered mental status = heat stroke. Sweating is often preserved in exertional heat stroke (a common exam trap — anhidrosis is NOT required for diagnosis). Reference: Tintinalli p.1348.

8. Diagnosis & Differential

Tintinalli Table 210-2 p.1348

Heat stroke is a clinical diagnosis — established by hyperthermia + altered mental status + heat exposure history, AFTER excluding alternative causes.

Workup priorities

  • Rectal/esophageal core temperature (NOT oral, axillary, tympanic — unreliable).
  • Fingerstick glucose — exclude hypoglycemia.
  • CBC, full chemistry panel, LFT, CK, lactate — assess multi-organ involvement.
  • Coagulation panel — DIC develops rapidly.
  • ABG / lactate — metabolic acidosis common.
  • UA — myoglobinuria.
  • ECG — sinus tachycardia, T-wave changes; identify dysrhythmia.
  • CT head + LP if focal neurologic features or to exclude meningitis.
  • Toxicology screen — exclude sympathomimetic, anticholinergic, salicylate ingestion.
📚 Table 210-2 — Differential Diagnosis of Heat Stroke (Tintinalli p.1348)

Critical mimics to exclude

  • Sepsis with hyperthermia — meningitis, encephalitis, typhoid, malaria.
  • Toxicological: anticholinergic toxidrome, sympathomimetic, salicylate, MDMA, cocaine.
  • Endocrine: thyroid storm, pheochromocytoma, diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Neurologic: intracranial hemorrhage, status epilepticus, cerebrovascular accident, hypothalamic injury.
  • Drug-induced hyperthermia syndromes: serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS), malignant hyperthermia, withdrawal syndromes.
Distinguish from NMS / serotonin syndrome: Both have hyperthermia + AMS + autonomic instability. Heat stroke usually lacks rigidity; NMS has lead-pipe rigidity; serotonin syndrome has clonus + hyperreflexia. Heat stroke is associated with environmental exposure history.
Inline MCQ 4. Which method is preferred for measuring core body temperature in a suspected heat stroke patient?
A. Tympanic infrared
B. Oral thermometer
C. Rectal probe (or esophageal/bladder)
D. Axillary
Answer: C. Rectal probe (or esophageal/bladder for intubated) — surface measurements unreliable in shock and during cooling. Reference: Tintinalli p.1348.

9. Cooling Techniques

Tintinalli Table 210-3 p.1349

Aggressive cooling is the cornerstone — aim to lower core temperature to ≤39°C (102°F) within 30 minutes, then continue to normothermia.

📚 Table 210-3 — Summary of Cooling Techniques: Advantages and Disadvantages (Tintinalli p.1349)

Methods compared

MethodHow it worksUse case
Evaporative cooling (recommended)Tepid water mist (15°C) + fans → rapid heat loss via evaporationEasy, well-tolerated, broadly applicable; first-line in classic heat stroke
Immersion coolingCold water (or ice slurry) tub immersionAthletes, exertional heat stroke; fastest cooling rate
Ice packs (groin, neck, axillae)Direct conduction to large vesselsAdjunct only — slow when used alone
Cold IV crystalloidInternal heat exchange (~4°C fluid)Adjunct; avoids fluid overload concern in elderly
Cold gastric/bladder/peritoneal lavageInternal cooling via cold fluid lavageSevere cases; rarely used in ED

Cardiopulmonary bypass / ECMOMost rapid; full circulatory supportRefractory severe cases

Practical pearls

  • Stop cooling at ~39°C (102°F) to avoid overshoot hypothermia.
  • Shivering generates heat — control with benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam 5–10 mg IV) if needed.
  • Antipyretics (acetaminophen, aspirin) are INEFFECTIVE — heat stroke is not pyrogenic. Worse, they may exacerbate coagulopathy and hepatic injury.
  • Dantrolene is NOT indicated — reserve for malignant hyperthermia and severe NMS.
  • Continuous core temperature monitoring during cooling — rapid changes may be missed.
Antipyretics in heat stroke: contraindicated or futile. Acetaminophen does not lower set-point that's not raised; aspirin can worsen hepatic dysfunction and bleeding. Cool by physical methods only.

Hepatic injury is a hallmark of severe heat stroke — peaks 24–72 h after onset. Almost all patients with severe heat stroke have some degree of liver injury. Severe cases may progress to fulminant hepatic failure requiring transplantation.
Rhabdomyolysis + AKI — common in exertional heat stroke. Aggressive IV crystalloid + electrolyte monitoring; alkalinization with NaHCO₃ controversial. Watch K⁺, Ca²⁺, phosphate.

11. Disposition

Tintinalli p.1350
  • Heat cramps: discharge home after rest + electrolyte replacement.
  • Heat exhaustion: ED observation 4–6 h with rehydration; discharge if normal vitals + mental status; counsel on prevention and follow-up.
  • Heat stroke: ICU admission for ≥48–72 h:
    • Continuous core temperature monitoring
    • Hourly UOP, CK, electrolytes, LFT, coagulation
    • Mechanical ventilation if respiratory failure / coma
    • Hemodialysis if AKI / refractory hyperkalemia
    • Watch for delayed multi-organ failure
  • Discharge counseling: avoid future heat exposure for 1–2 weeks; risk of recurrence is high in the same season.

⚕️ ATLS 11th — Heat Illness in Trauma

ATLS — Special Considerations
  • Trauma patients with prolonged outdoor exposure are at risk for combined hyperthermic and hemorrhagic shock — both decrease tissue perfusion.
  • Aggressive cooling in hyperthermic trauma patients while continuing volume resuscitation; avoid LR if rhabdomyolysis suspected.
  • Coagulopathy in heat stroke is similar to trauma-induced coagulopathy — replace fibrinogen, platelets, FFP per ratios.
  • Sedation for shivering during cooling — benzodiazepines preferred; avoid succinylcholine if hyperkalemia from rhabdo.
  • Mental status changes in trauma + heat exposure → broaden differential to include head injury, intoxication, and metabolic causes.
  • Heat-injured trauma patients require ICU admission regardless of trauma severity, due to risk of delayed multi-organ failure.

📗 Rosen's EM 10th — Heat Illness

Rosen's Ch.132
  • Same overall framework — emphasis on cold-water immersion as fastest cooling method for exertional heat stroke (cooling rate ~0.2°C/min vs evaporative ~0.05–0.1°C/min).
  • Rosen's notes that classical heat stroke patients (elderly) often do not tolerate immersion well due to comorbidities, agitation, monitoring difficulty — evaporative remains preferred for them.
  • Target temperature: 39°C (102°F), not normothermia, to avoid overshoot.
  • Rosen's cautions against cooling rate >0.3°C/min due to risk of arrhythmia and overshoot hypothermia.
  • NMS / serotonin syndrome / malignant hyperthermia distinction — Rosen's provides detailed comparative table; key bedside differentiator is rigidity (NMS = lead pipe; serotonin = clonus/hyperreflexia; MH = succinylcholine/anesthetic exposure).
  • Pediatric and elderly populations require lower temperature thresholds for concern (children deteriorate faster; elderly have less reserve).
  • Long-term prognosis: mortality 10–50% for heat stroke depending on duration of hyperthermia; survivors may have persistent cognitive deficits and increased lifelong vulnerability to heat illness.

🔑 Key Points (High-Yield)

  1. Heat stroke triad: core temp >40°C + altered mental status + heat exposure history. Anhidrosis NOT required.
  2. Classic vs Exertional: classic in elderly with comorbidities + anhidrosis; exertional in young athletes with preserved sweating.
  3. Pathophysiology: evaporation is the primary defense above 35°C ambient — humidity blocks it.
  4. Heat cramps = relative hyponatremia from hypotonic fluid replacement during sweating.
  5. Heat exhaustion vs Heat stroke: AMS + temp >40°C separates the two — treat aggressively if either is present.
  6. Cold-water immersion = fastest cooling for exertional. Evaporative for classic / elderly.
  7. Stop cooling at 39°C to avoid overshoot.
  8. Antipyretics (acetaminophen, aspirin) are ineffective and may worsen hepatic injury / DIC.
  9. Benzodiazepines for shivering during cooling.
  10. Differential: NMS (rigidity), serotonin syndrome (clonus + hyperreflexia), malignant hyperthermia (anesthetic), thyroid storm, sepsis, sympathomimetic toxicity.
  11. Hepatic injury peaks 24–72 h post — almost universal in severe cases.
  12. ICU admission for heat stroke; monitor for delayed multi-organ failure (DIC, ARDS, AKI, hepatic failure).

Toxicology Chapter 206

Antimicrobials

Most antimicrobial exposures are not dramatic overdoses. The board-relevant patient is the one with seizures, QT/QRS toxicity, methemoglobinemia, renal injury, serotonin syndrome, or isoniazid-induced refractory seizures.

Board lens: do not memorize every antibiotic. Pattern-match neurotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, dyshemoglobinemia, renal injury, and high-yield antidotes.

Core Toxicities

Tintinalli Ch206 uses one major table as the spine. It ties the dangerous antimicrobial families to the specific ED treatment: benzodiazepines for many seizure syndromes, methylene blue for methemoglobinemia, magnesium for torsades, and high-dose pyridoxine for isoniazid.

Antibacterials

Beta-lactams usually cause GI symptoms in overdose, but large penicillin or cephalosporin exposure can cause seizures, especially with renal dysfunction. Amoxicillin can cause crystalluria, hematuria, and acute renal failure; the practical treatment is IV fluids.

Fluoroquinolones can cause seizures and QT prolongation, and adult tendon rupture risk is highest with levofloxacin. Macrolides and ketolides matter because QT prolongation and torsades are the board trap; give magnesium for torsades and avoid stacking other QT-prolongers.

Medication interaction trap: linezolid is a reversible MAO inhibitor. With SSRIs/SNRIs or other serotonergic drugs, think serotonin syndrome, not just antibiotic side effect.

Vancomycin red man syndrome is an anaphylactoid infusion-rate reaction with flushing, pruritus, chest pain, dyspnea, and sometimes hypotension. Stop or slow the infusion and give antihistamines; severe true anaphylaxis is a different pathway.

Rosen source check

Rosen Ch.141 - linezolid and serotonin syndrome

Rosen lists linezolid among drugs capable of producing serotonin syndrome and emphasizes the triad of mental status change, autonomic instability, and neuromuscular hyperactivity.

Rosen vs Tintinalli

Tintinalli names linezolid's MAO inhibition; Rosen gives the bedside toxidrome frame.

Decision change

Ask about SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, tramadol, meperidine, lithium, dextromethorphan, and stimulant coexposure.

Antimalarials

Chloroquine is the high-danger antimalarial: toxicity can start within hours with GI symptoms, seizures, QRS/QT prolongation, hypotension, respiratory depression, and hypokalemia. Treat aggressively with airway control, benzodiazepines, and vasopressor support when needed.

Quinine/quinidine can cause sodium-potassium channel blockade, alpha-adrenergic antagonism, hypoglycemia, ototoxicity, and ophthalmic toxicity. Quinine is one of the few drugs where multiple-dose activated charcoal is truly indicated.

Rosen source check

Rosen Box 135.12 - MDAC and ABCDQ

Rosen's poisoned-patient chapter lists quinine and dapsone among the small group of substances for which multiple-dose activated charcoal can enhance elimination.

Rosen vs Tintinalli

Tintinalli identifies quinine-specific therapy; Rosen reinforces that MDAC is not a generic detox move but a targeted elimination strategy.

Decision change

Consider MDAC early for serious quinine/dapsone exposure if airway and GI safety allow.

Primaquine is a methemoglobin/hemolysis drug, especially in G6PD deficiency. If cyanosis or low saturation does not fit the PaO2, check co-oximetry and treat significant methemoglobinemia with methylene blue unless contraindicated.

Antituberculous Medications

Isoniazid is the must-know drug. Acute overdose causes seizures, metabolic acidosis, and protracted coma. Seizures may be refractory to standard anticonvulsants because INH causes functional pyridoxine deficiency and impaired GABA synthesis.

Do this fast: benzodiazepines plus pyridoxine gram-for-gram. If the amount is unknown, adults get 5 g IV; children get 70 mg/kg IV up to 5 g.

Phenytoin has no role in INH seizures. After seizures stop, give the remaining pyridoxine over 4 to 6 hours. Most toxicity declares within 2 hours; patients asymptomatic after 6 hours of ED observation are generally safe for medical clearance.

Ethambutol is mainly ocular toxicity. Rifampin can cause orange/red discoloration, GI symptoms, and rarely severe toxicity with renal injury. Pyrazinamide is not expected to cause major acute overdose toxicity.

Antifungals, Antiparasitics, and Antiviral/Retroviral Medications

Triazole antifungals rarely produce severe acute overdose but can cause hepatotoxicity and inhibit CYP3A4. Amphotericin B toxicity is not subtle: fever, rigors, hypotension, tachycardia, anemia, electrolyte wasting, nephrotoxicity, neuropathy, and cardiac arrest are possible.

Antiparasitics are usually minimally toxic in acute overdose, but levamisole-adulterated cocaine can cause agranulocytosis and vasculitis. Oseltamivir commonly causes nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, dizziness, and headache; rare neuropsychiatric effects may appear within 24 hours.

Antiretrovirals matter through interaction and chronic toxicity: protease inhibitors can cause QT prolongation and nephrolithiasis; reverse transcriptase inhibitors can cause lipodystrophy and lactic acidosis. Methadone/buprenorphine interactions require database or specialist checking.

Inline MCQs

1. A patient ingests a massive penicillin dose and has seizures. Best specific symptomatic therapy?
Rationale: Tintinalli Table 206-1 lists seizures from large penicillin exposure and benzodiazepines as symptomatic therapy.
2. Amoxicillin overdose is most associated with which renal finding?
Rationale: Tintinalli emphasizes crystalluria/hematuria/acute renal failure and IV fluids.
3. Cephalosporin toxicity in renal failure most commonly presents as:
Rationale: Cephalosporins, especially with renal impairment/high doses, may cause encephalopathy and seizures.
4. Which antimicrobial toxicity is matched with methylene blue in Tintinalli Table 206-1?
Rationale: Sulfonamides may produce methemoglobinemia; methylene blue is listed as specific therapy.
5. Vancomycin red man syndrome is best managed by:
Rationale: It is an anaphylactoid infusion-rate reaction; stop/slow infusion and antihistamines usually work.
6. Linezolid is risky with SSRIs because it can cause:
Rationale: Tintinalli notes linezolid inhibits monoamine oxidase and can precipitate serotonin syndrome with serotonergic drugs; Rosen also lists linezolid in serotonin syndrome causes.

Assessment

7. Macrolide toxicity is most board-relevant because it can:
Rationale: Macrolides can prolong QT and cause torsades; magnesium is the acute torsades therapy.
8. Fluoroquinolone overdose rarely kills, but the key ED risks are:
Rationale: Tintinalli lists seizures and QT issues; fluoroquinolones also have renal/tendon and dysglycemia warnings.
9. Chloroquine toxicity should be considered dangerous because it may cause:
Rationale: Chloroquine can cause rapid cardiovascular collapse and neurotoxicity.
10. For severe chloroquine toxicity, Tintinalli emphasizes:
Rationale: Chloroquine treatment is aggressive supportive care with benzodiazepines and vasopressor support when needed.
11. Quinine overdose is one of the few overdoses where Rosen highlights:
Rationale: Rosen Box 135.12 lists quinine among ABCDQ drugs amenable to multiple-dose activated charcoal.
12. Quinine/quinidine overdose can produce:
Rationale: Tintinalli lists sodium-potassium channel blockade, alpha-adrenergic antagonism, hypoglycemia, ototoxicity, and ophthalmic toxicity.
13. Primaquine toxicity should raise concern for:
Rationale: Tintinalli lists primaquine methemoglobinemia/hemolysis; G6PD deficiency increases hemolysis risk.
14. The classic isoniazid overdose triad is:
Rationale: Tintinalli states classic acute INH overdose features are seizures, metabolic acidosis, and protracted coma.
15. Specific therapy for isoniazid-induced seizures is:
Rationale: Pyridoxine treats functional B6 deficiency from INH; benzodiazepines manage seizures.
16. Unknown quantity INH overdose in an adult: recommended pyridoxine dose?
Rationale: Tintinalli recommends 5 g IV in adults when the ingested amount is unknown.
17. Known INH amount: pyridoxine dosing is:
Rationale: Tintinalli: pyridoxine dose is gram-for-gram equivalent to the amount of INH ingested.
18. Phenytoin for INH seizures is:
Rationale: Tintinalli says phenytoin has no role in seizures from INH overdose.
19. Which antimicrobial adverse effect is linked to C. difficile risk?
Rationale: Clindamycin is among antibiotics carrying higher risk for C. difficile and pseudomembranous colitis.
20. Ethambutol toxicity is primarily:
Rationale: Tintinalli lists ethambutol ocular toxicity with blurred vision and color perception disruption.
21. Rifampin overdose can cause:
Rationale: Tintinalli notes rifampin can produce orange/red discoloration, GI symptoms, and acute toxicity including kidney injury.
22. Oseltamivir is most often associated with:
Rationale: Tintinalli lists common GI/neurologic symptoms and rare neuropsychiatric effects.
23. Protease inhibitors as a class can cause:
Rationale: Tintinalli notes protease inhibitors can cause QT prolongation and nephrolithiasis.
24. Which antimicrobial can interact with buprenorphine levels?
Rationale: Tintinalli mentions a maintained list of antiretrovirals that affect buprenorphine levels.
25. Disposition after INH exposure: asymptomatic patients after ED presentation are generally safe after:
Rationale: Tintinalli: most INH toxicity occurs within 2 hours; asymptomatic patients after 6 hours are safe for medical clearance.
26. Best global ED approach to antimicrobial overdose is:
Rationale: Tintinalli: most remain asymptomatic and observation is often all that is required; testing and treatments are based on agent and clinical condition.

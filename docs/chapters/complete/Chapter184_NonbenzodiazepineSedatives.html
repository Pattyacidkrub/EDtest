Toxicology Chapter 184

Nonbenzodiazepine Sedatives

This is not one toxidrome. These agents share sedation, but the board task is to spot agent-specific danger: chloral dysrhythmias, GHB rapid coma, carisoprodol myoclonus, and meprobamate bezoar.

Board frame: supportive care first, then identify the named product and its red flags.

Drug Map and Overdose Features

Nonbenzodiazepine sedatives include buspirone, carisoprodol/meprobamate, chloral hydrate, GHB and precursors, melatonin agonists, and Z-drugs. Most overdoses are managed with monitoring and supportive care, but chloral hydrate and GHB are the most testable high-risk patterns.

Buspirone

Buspirone is a 5-HT1A partial agonist used for anxiety. Overdose usually causes sedation, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and mild CNS effects. Rare generalized seizures and serotonin syndrome have been reported, so hyperreflexia, clonus, hyperthermia, or agitation should push the differential beyond simple sedation.

Carisoprodol and Meprobamate

Carisoprodol is metabolized to meprobamate. Toxicity can include sedation, coma, cardiovascular collapse, pulmonary edema, and myoclonic jerks. Meprobamate can cause cardiopulmonary depression and large ingestions may form a gastric bezoar with prolonged coma.

Management is supportive: airway, ventilation, hemodynamics, and evaluation for coingestants. Selected prolonged severe meprobamate cases may need toxicology and GI consultation for bezoar strategy.

Chloral Hydrate

Chloral hydrate is an older sedative with a narrow therapeutic index. Overdose causes coma and can produce cardiac instability, ventricular dysrhythmias, sensitivity to catecholamines, GI distress, and hemorrhagic gastritis. Ethanol coingestion potentiates sedation and alters metabolism.

Treatment is supportive with ECG monitoring. Treat torsades with magnesium and standard resuscitation. Hypotension and unstable dysrhythmias may require ICU care and toxicology consultation.

GHB, GBL, and 1,4-Butanediol

GHB has a steep dose-response curve and short half-life. Patients may present with sudden coma, bradycardia, hypoventilation, vomiting, aspiration risk, seizures, and then sudden awakening. Routine urine drug screens do not detect GHB; forensic or confirmatory testing must be collected early when needed.

GBL and 1,4-butanediol convert to GHB and can produce similar or prolonged toxicity. Management is supportive airway and ventilation care. Physostigmine is not recommended. GHB withdrawal after chronic frequent use can be severe, with agitation, delirium, hallucinations, autonomic instability, seizures, rhabdomyolysis, and hyperthermia.

Zolpidem, Zaleplon, Zopiclone, Eszopiclone, and Melatonin Agents

Z-drug overdoses usually cause sedation and impaired coordination; isolated fatalities are uncommon and typically involve coingestants. Zolpidem can cause vomiting and complex behaviors. Zaleplon may rarely cause blue-green discoloration from capsule dye. Zopiclone/eszopiclone usually cause sedation, with rare reports of methemoglobinemia or hemolytic anemia after large zopiclone overdose.

Melatonin, ramelteon, and tasimelteon generally produce mild sedation or disorientation. Treatment is observation and supportive care.

Disposition

Observe until mental status, respiratory status, ambulation, and vital signs are stable. Admit for persistent CNS or respiratory depression, aspiration, unstable dysrhythmia, significant chloral hydrate ingestion, severe GHB withdrawal, concerning coingestants, trauma/assault concern, or intentional ingestion requiring psychiatric evaluation.

Do not miss: drug-facilitated assault concerns require trauma-informed care and early specimen collection because GHB detection windows are short.

1. Introduction & Epidemiology

Tintinalli Ch.212 p.1358
  • Worldwide: ~2.7 million envenomations per year, ~100,000 deaths.
  • US: 6,000–8,000 venomous bites annually; ~5–6 deaths.
  • Two major venomous families in the US:
    • Viperidae (subfamily Crotalinae — pit vipers): rattlesnakes (Crotalus), cottonmouths/water moccasins (Agkistrodon piscivorus), copperheads (Agkistrodon contortrix), pygmy rattlesnakes/massasauga (Sistrurus) — >95% of US bites.
    • Elapidae: coral snakes (Micrurus) — <5% of US bites.
  • Most US envenomations are non-fatal with prompt antivenom.
  • Mortality is highest in rural/remote regions, in children, and with delayed presentation.

2. Pit Vipers (Crotalinae)

Tintinalli p.1358

Anatomy & Identification

  • Heat-sensing pits between eye and nostril (loreal pit) — pathognomonic of pit vipers.
  • Elliptical (vertical-slit) pupils — vs round pupils in non-venomous and elapids.
  • Triangular head, broader than the neck.
  • Rattlesnake: terminal rattle of keratin segments.
📚 Fig 212-1 — Pit viper head anatomy: elliptical pupil, nostril, heat-sensing loreal pit (Tintinalli p.1358)

Pathophysiology

  • Crotaline venom = complex enzyme mix → local tissue injury (hemorrhagic, necrotic) + systemic effects (coagulopathy, hemolysis, vascular permeability, neuromuscular dysfunction).
  • Causes vessel wall damage and consumes fibrinogen and platelets — leading to a thrombocytopenia–hypofibrinogenemia–coagulopathy picture without DIC fragmentation.
  • Rare neurotoxic effects with some species (Mojave rattlesnake — Mojave toxin A → presynaptic nerve terminal block → respiratory failure).

"Dry bites"

  • Up to 25% of pit viper bites involve no envenomation ("dry bite") — fang puncture without venom injection.
  • Observe ≥6–8 hours with serial exam + labs before deciding the bite was dry.
Pearl — bite ≠ envenomation: All snakebites need observation. Early absence of symptoms does not exclude envenomation; significant local injury can develop over the first 6–8 hours.

3. Clinical Features of Pit Viper Envenomation

Tintinalli p.1358–1359

Severity Grading

SeverityLocalSystemicCoagulation
Minimal (no envenomation)Bite marks; no swelling/painNoneNormal
MildPain + edema confined to bite siteNoneNormal
ModerateEdema progresses beyond bite site; ecchymosisNausea, vomiting, weakness, paresthesias, mild HoTNMild abnormalities (↓ fibrinogen, ↓ platelets, ↑ INR)
SevereFull extremity edema; bullae; necrosisHypotension, altered mental status, respiratory distress, shockSevere coagulopathy with hemorrhagic complications

Local features

  • Fang marks (1–4 puncture wounds; sometimes a single mark).
  • Pain, swelling within minutes; edema progresses over hours.
  • Erythema, ecchymosis, bullae, necrosis with severe envenomation.

Systemic features

  • Cardiovascular: hypotension, tachycardia, shock from third-spacing and capillary leak.
  • Respiratory: respiratory distress, paralysis (Mojave toxin).
  • Hematologic: thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia, prolonged PT/PTT, hemorrhage (gum bleeding, hematuria, hematemesis, intracranial bleeding).
  • Neuromuscular: myokymia (rippling muscle movements), fasciculations, paresthesias, metallic taste.
  • Mental status: altered (severe cases).

4. First Aid & Prehospital Management

Tintinalli Table 212-1 p.1359
📚 Table 212-1 — Recommended First Aid Measures (Tintinalli p.1359)

What to DO

  1. Retreat well beyond the strike range (snakes can strike up to half their body length).
  2. Calm + immobilize the patient; keep the bitten extremity at heart level (not above, not below).
  3. Remove constricting items (rings, watches, tight clothing) — edema progresses rapidly.
  4. Mark the leading edge of swelling with a pen and timestamp; mark every 15 min.
  5. Rapid transport to a facility with antivenom.
  6. Photograph the snake from a safe distance if possible — but DO NOT attempt to capture it.

What NOT to DO

⚠️ AVOID:
• Tourniquets (arterial occlusion → ischemia, worse local damage)
• "Cut and suck" (no proven benefit; introduces infection)
• Suction devices (Sawyer Extractor) — worsen tissue damage
• Ice/cold packs (cause vasoconstriction → worse necrosis)
• Electric shock therapy — no benefit, may cause harm
• Alcohol/aspirin consumption — worsen bleeding
• Pressure-immobilization bandage for North American pit vipers — venom is cytotoxic; concentrating it locally worsens necrosis. (Pressure-immobilization is appropriate for elapids and Australian funnel-web spider, NOT for pit vipers.)

Constriction band (controversial — only if antivenom not available within reasonable time)

  • Wide elastic bandage at very loose tension (allow 1–2 fingers under) above the bite, restricting lymphatic but NOT arterial flow.
  • Apply between bite and heart, not over the bite itself.
  • Maintain capillary refill, distal pulses.
  • If bandage is in place on arrival, leave alone until antivenom administered.

5. ED Evaluation & Severity Grading

Tintinalli Tables 212-2, 212-3 p.1359–1360

Diagnosis

  • Diagnosis based on (1) history of snake exposure, (2) presence of fang marks, and (3) progressive symptoms.
  • Photograph helps but identification is not required to start treatment.
📚 Table 212-2 — Clinical Features and Treatment by Reptile Type (Tintinalli p.1359)

Lab Workup

📚 Table 212-3 — Laboratory Evaluation in Crotaline or Elapid Snakebite (Tintinalli p.1360)
  • CBC, INR/PT, PTT, fibrinogen, fibrin degradation products — repeat at 6, 12, 24 h.
  • BUN/Cr, electrolytes, glucose, CK, urinalysis — assess organ injury.
  • ABG if respiratory distress.
  • ECG, type & cross if hemorrhage.
  • Compartment pressures if compartment syndrome suspected.
Pearl — Recurrent coagulopathy: after CroFab treatment, recurrent thrombocytopenia and hypofibrinogenemia may develop 2–14 days post-discharge. Counsel patients to return for any easy bruising, bleeding, or for re-checking labs at 2 days post-discharge.

6. Crotaline Antivenom — CroFab (FabAV)

Tintinalli Fig 212-2 p.1360

Indications

  • Progressive local effects (advancing edema, ecchymosis, bullae).
  • Systemic effects (hypotension, AMS, respiratory distress).
  • Hematologic abnormalities (thrombocytopenia <100,000, fibrinogen <150 mg/dL, prolonged INR/PT/PTT).
📚 Fig 212-2 — General strategy for CroFab antivenom: establish initial control, then maintenance dosing (Tintinalli p.1360)

Dosing — CroFab (Crotalidae Polyvalent Immune Fab, ovine-derived)

  1. Initial control: 4–6 vials IV over 1 h. Reassess at 1 h.
  2. If not at "initial control" (no further progression of local + systemic + lab effects), give additional 4-vial doses every 1 h until control achieved.
  3. Maintenance: 2-vial doses at 6, 12, and 18 h after initial control achieved.
  4. Pediatric dose is the same as adult — don't reduce by weight.
  5. Premixed in NS over 1 h. Slow rate if initial signs of allergic reaction.

Adverse reactions

  • Allergic reaction (urticaria, bronchospasm) — slow rate; treat with antihistamines, steroids, epinephrine.
  • Serum sickness — appears 7–21 days post-treatment; type III hypersensitivity (rash, arthralgia, fever); steroids and antihistamines.

Crotalidae Immune F(ab')₂ — Anavip

  • Anavip (equine F(ab')₂) — alternative to CroFab. Longer half-life → less risk of recurrent coagulopathy.
  • Dosing: 10 vials IV over 1 h initial; 4-vial doses every 1 h until control; no scheduled maintenance dosing required (vs CroFab's 2-vial maintenance).
Pearl — North American snakes covered: CroFab/FabAV/Anavip cover all native US Crotalinae (rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths). Coral snake bites need a different antivenom.
Inline MCQ 4. Initial CroFab dosing for envenomation is:
A. 1–2 vials IV bolus
B. 4–6 vials IV over 1 hour
C. 20 vials IV bolus
D. Half adult dose for pediatric
Answer: B. Initial control: 4–6 vials over 1 hour, then 2-vial maintenance at 6, 12, 18 h. Pediatric dose = adult — do not reduce. Reference: Tintinalli Fig 212-2.

7. Compartment Syndrome from Crotaline Envenomation

Tintinalli Table 212-4 p.1360
📚 Table 212-4 — Management of Compartment Syndrome from Crotaline Envenomation (Tintinalli p.1360)

Diagnosis

  • Pain out of proportion to exam, tense compartment, paresthesias, paralysis (late).
  • Measure intracompartmental pressure — >30 mm Hg generally concerning.

Management algorithm

  1. Elevate limb.
  2. Mannitol 1–2 g/kg IV over 30 min (osmotic effect to reduce edema).
  3. Additional antivenom over 60 min — frequently resolves rising pressure.
  4. Fasciotomy ONLY if pressure persists >60 min despite the above and clinical features confirm true compartment syndrome.
⚠️ Pitfall: Snakebite often LOOKS like compartment syndrome but is actually subcutaneous envenomation. Fasciotomy is rarely needed if antivenom is given promptly. Surgery without exhausting medical therapy is associated with worse outcomes.
Inline MCQ 5. A patient with a copperhead bite has tense edema and intracompartmental pressure 38 mm Hg 2 hours into CroFab. The most appropriate next step is:
A. Immediate fasciotomy
B. Stop CroFab and observe
C. Elevate limb + mannitol 1–2 g/kg IV + additional antivenom over 60 min
D. NSAIDs alone
Answer: C. Try medical management first: elevation + mannitol + extra antivenom. Fasciotomy reserved for persistent >30 mm Hg after 60 min of these measures. Reference: Tintinalli Table 212-4.

8. Elapid Snakebite

Tintinalli p.1361

US Coral Snakes

  • Eastern coral snake (Micrurus fulvius): Texas, Arizona, southeast US.
  • Texas coral snake (Micrurus tener) and Sonoran coral snake (Micruroides euryxanthus) — smaller envenomation potential.
  • Coral snakes account for <1% of US bites.
  • Identification mnemonic: "Red on yellow, kill a fellow; red on black, venom lack" — describes the order of bands. Note: this rule holds for North American coral snakes ONLY (not international species).

Pathophysiology — Elapid venom

  • Predominantly neurotoxic (vs Crotaline which is hemotoxic/cytotoxic).
  • Postsynaptic neuromuscular junction blockade (curare-like) → flaccid paralysis.
  • Local effects are typically mild → delayed presentation common.

Clinical features

  • Local: minimal pain, swelling, or tissue injury (deceiving!).
  • Systemic (delayed onset 6–12 h, may be even later):
    • Cranial nerve dysfunction: ptosis, dysphagia, dysarthria, diplopia.
    • Generalized weakness, muscle fasciculations.
    • Respiratory failure → can require prolonged mechanical ventilation.
    • Once respiratory failure occurs, recovery may take days–weeks.

Treatment

  • All suspected coral snake bites: admit and observe ≥24 h regardless of initial findings.
  • Antivenom (Micrurus fulvius antivenin):
    • If neurotoxic features present, give immediately.
    • Initial dose: 3–5 vials IV over 1 h.
    • Some authorities advocate empiric administration in any bite from M. fulvius regardless of symptoms.
  • Pressure-immobilization bandage appropriate for elapids (to slow neurotoxin systemic spread) — different from Crotalinae.
  • Supportive ventilation if respiratory paralysis develops; prepare for prolonged intubation.
Inline MCQ 6. Which feature distinguishes elapid (coral snake) envenomation from Crotaline?
A. Severe local necrosis at bite site
B. Coagulopathy
C. Mild local effects + delayed neurotoxic features (ptosis, dysphagia, respiratory failure)
D. Hemolysis
Answer: C. Coral snakes produce minimal local injury but delayed neurotoxicity (cranial nerve dysfunction, paralysis). Reference: Tintinalli p.1361.

Thai Antivenom Specifics

The Queen Saovabha Memorial Institute (QSMI / สถานเสาวภา) produces species-specific monovalent and polyvalent antivenoms used widely in Thailand and Southeast Asia.

  • Hemotoxic polyvalent (covers Malayan pit viper, white-lipped pit viper, Russell's viper, green pit viper).
  • Neurotoxic polyvalent (covers cobras, kraits).
  • Sea snake antivenom.
  • Monovalent products available for individual species when identification is certain.
Thai EM pearl — neurotoxic envenomation: Krait bite is often painless and overlooked at night; presents 6–12 h later with progressive paralysis. Always consider snake bite in patients with unexplained ptosis + cranial nerve dysfunction in endemic areas.

Treatment Approach in Thailand

  1. First aid: immobilize, mark swelling edge, do NOT use cut/suck/tourniquet.
  2. Pressure-immobilization bandage acceptable for neurotoxic bites (cobras, kraits, sea snakes) — slow venom dissemination.
  3. Lab workup: 20-minute whole-blood clotting test (20-WBCT) — bedside test for coagulopathy in resource-limited settings.
  4. Antivenom indications:
    • Coagulopathy by 20-WBCT or lab
    • Neurotoxic features (ptosis, paralysis)
    • Local progression
    • Systemic symptoms (hypotension, AKI from Russell's)
  5. Atropine + neostigmine — adjunct for postsynaptic neurotoxic envenomation (cobras) — improves muscle weakness.
  6. Hemodialysis for AKI (especially Russell's viper).

10. Disposition

Tintinalli p.1360
  • All venomous bites require admission for observation, with rare exceptions:
    • Discharge home only after ≥6–8 h of normal vitals + normal serial labs + no progression of swelling — strongly indicating dry bite.
    • Most US Crotaline bites: admit ≥24 h.
    • Coral snake bite: admit ≥24 h regardless of symptoms (delayed neurotoxicity).
    • Severe envenomation: ICU.
  • Discharge counseling:
    • Recurrent coagulopathy possible — return for bleeding/bruising; recheck labs at 2 days post-discharge.
    • Tetanus prophylaxis per immunization status.
    • Wound care: signs of infection, repeat eval at 24 h.
    • Consult poison control: 1-800-222-1222 (US) or local equivalent.

⚕️ ATLS 11th — Snakebite in Trauma

ATLS
  • Snakebite in trauma patients: rule out coexisting traumatic injury (envenomated patients may also fall, drive into objects).
  • Coagulopathy from venom + traumatic hemorrhage = potentiates bleeding. Manage both: correct fibrinogen (cryoprecipitate), platelets, FFP.
  • Tourniquets for hemorrhage control acceptable in trauma (different from snakebite first-aid context).
  • Compartment syndrome common postvenom — measure pressures; medical management before fasciotomy.
  • Antivenom + tetanus + antibiotics for contaminated wound + supportive trauma care.
  • Coral snake or krait + trauma → high suspicion for delayed paralysis; consider intubation early if mental status declining.
  • Avoid succinylcholine if rhabdomyolysis suspected (sea snake, Russell's viper) — hyperkalemia risk.

📗 Rosen's EM 10th — Snakebite

Rosen's Ch.55 (Snakebites)
  • Aligns with Tintinalli on Crotalinae: CroFab/FabAV first-line; observation 6–8 h for dry bite; 24-h admission for envenomation.
  • Anavip (F(ab')₂) — Rosen's notes its longer half-life makes it preferred when available, reducing risk of recurrent coagulopathy.
  • Recurrent coagulopathy: emphasized in Rosen's — counsel patients on signs of bleeding 5–14 days post-discharge.
  • Pediatric dosing identical to adult — do NOT weight-adjust antivenom.
  • Fasciotomy rarely needed; only after maximizing medical therapy (mannitol + additional antivenom).
  • International species: Rosen's includes detailed sections on Australian elapids, African mambas, viperids, sea snakes — antivenom availability and pressure-immobilization first aid.
  • Allergy to antivenom: skin testing not routinely recommended; monitor during infusion; have epi/diphenhydramine ready.
  • "Pressure immobilization" — Rosen's reserves this for elapids (predominantly neurotoxic); explicitly advises AGAINST for North American pit vipers.

  3. CroFab (FabAV): 4–6 vials IV initial control + 2-vial maintenance at 6, 12, 18 h. Pediatric = adult dose.
  4. Anavip (F(ab')₂): longer half-life, no scheduled maintenance — alternative.
  5. Pressure-immobilization for elapids only (neurotoxic); NOT for North American pit vipers.
  6. AVOID: tourniquets, cut/suck, suction devices, ice, electric shock, alcohol, ASA.
  7. Compartment syndrome: mannitol + additional antivenom + elevation BEFORE fasciotomy. Fasciotomy rarely needed.
  8. Coral snake (US): minimal local effects, delayed neurotoxicity (ptosis, dysphagia, respiratory paralysis); admit ≥24 h.
  9. "Red on yellow, kill a fellow; red on black, venom lack" — North American coral snakes only.
  10. Recurrent coagulopathy 2–14 days post-CroFab — counsel patients to recheck labs at 2 days.
  11. Thailand QSMI antivenoms: Hemato polyvalent (vipers), Neuro polyvalent (cobras/kraits), Sea snake, Monovalents.
  12. Krait bite (Thailand) often painless + nocturnal + delayed paralysis 6–12 h → high suspicion in unexplained ptosis.
  13. Russell's viper — hemotoxic + nephrotoxic AKI; dialysis often needed.
  14. Cobra postsynaptic block — atropine + neostigmine adjunct (not for krait — presynaptic).
  15. 20-WBCT — bedside coagulation test for resource-limited SE Asia settings.

1. Definitions — Sepsis-3 (2016)

Tintinalli Ch.151 p.998

The Third International Consensus Definitions ("Sepsis-3") fundamentally redefined sepsis in 2016, removing the SIRS-based criteria and emphasizing organ dysfunction.

📚 Table 151-1 — Sepsis-3 Definitions of Sepsis and Septic Shock (Tintinalli p.998)
TermDefinition (Sepsis-3)
InfectionInvasion of normally sterile tissue by organisms.
BacteremiaBacteria in bloodstream — may or may not produce sepsis.
SepsisLife-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Suspected/proven infection + ↑ SOFA ≥2 from baseline.
Septic ShockSubset of sepsis with profound circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities → high mortality. Clinically: Sepsis + need for vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 + lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids.
📌 What changed from old definitions? "Severe sepsis" was eliminated as a category. SIRS criteria (HR, RR, WBC, temp) are no longer required to define sepsis but remain useful for screening. Sepsis-3 emphasizes organ dysfunction (SOFA score) over inflammatory criteria.

Why is this important for EM?

  • Sepsis remains a leading cause of in-hospital mortality (~30% for septic shock).
  • Time-sensitive condition — every hour of delayed antibiotics ↑ mortality ~7%.
  • Surviving Sepsis Campaign (2018) 1-h bundle is the standard ED practice.

Inline MCQ 1. Which is required for the diagnosis of septic shock per Sepsis-3 (2016) definitions?

2. Pathophysiology

Tintinalli p.998–999

Sepsis represents a dysregulated host response — the immune system overreacts or underreacts to infection, producing widespread tissue injury.

Key pathologic processes

  • Inflammatory cascade: cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) → endothelial dysfunction → vasodilation + capillary leak.
  • Coagulation cascade activation: microvascular thrombosis + DIC in severe cases.
  • Mitochondrial dysfunction: impaired oxidative phosphorylation despite adequate O₂ delivery → "cellular dysoxia."
  • Distributive shock physiology: ↓ SVR, normal-to-↑ CO early, late progression to septic cardiomyopathy with reduced EF.
  • Hypovolemia from capillary leak + insensible loss + venodilation.
📚 Fig 151-2 — Pathogenic sequence of events in septic shock (Tintinalli p.999)
"Warm" vs "cold" shock: Early sepsis often presents with warm extremities, bounding pulses, wide pulse pressure (high CO + low SVR). As shock progresses or in pediatric/elderly patients, may transition to "cold" shock with cool extremities, vasoconstriction, low CO — septic cardiomyopathy.
Inline MCQ 2. A patient with sepsis has tissue hypoperfusion despite adequate oxygen delivery (SpO₂ 99%, normal PaO₂, Hgb 12 g/dL, CO normal). The most likely explanation is:
A. Anemia
B. Hypoxemia
C. Mitochondrial dysfunction (cellular dysoxia)
D. Hypovolemia alone
Answer: C. "Cellular dysoxia" — impaired oxygen utilization at mitochondrial level despite adequate delivery is a hallmark of severe sepsis. Reference: Tintinalli p.998.

Atypical presentations to remember:
  • Elderly: may present with confusion + falls, no fever — "cryptic septic shock" with normal vitals but elevated lactate.
  • Immunocompromised/neutropenic: may not mount fever or leukocytosis.
  • Diabetic patients with foot/UTI infections — diabetic ketoacidosis may co-exist.
  • Pediatric: mottled skin, prolonged cap refill, lethargy — fever may be inconsistent.

C. Normal WBC

Answer: B. "Cryptic septic shock" — elevated lactate with apparently normal vitals signals tissue hypoperfusion. Elderly may not mount typical sepsis vitals. Reference: Tintinalli p.999.

4. Diagnosis & Workup

Tintinalli p.999–1001

ED workup checklist

  • Vital signs with continuous monitoring; serial assessments.
  • Lactate (immediate; repeat 2–4 h after initial resuscitation).
  • Blood cultures × 2 (different sites, before antibiotics if it does not delay therapy >45 min).
  • Source-specific cultures: urine, sputum, wound, CSF, abscess, joint fluid as indicated.
  • CBC, full chemistry, LFTs, lipase, coagulation panel (PT/INR/aPTT, fibrinogen).
  • Procalcitonin — adjunct to support bacterial vs viral; NOT used to withhold antibiotics in suspected sepsis.
  • ABG if respiratory distress or shock.
  • Imaging: chest X-ray; CT/US guided by suspected source (abdomen, pelvis, soft tissue).
  • ECG if cardiac symptoms or atrial fibrillation common in sepsis.
  • Bedside US: assess volume status (IVC), cardiac function, free fluid, pulmonary B-lines.
Lactate interpretation:
• <2 mmol/L: normal
• 2–4 mmol/L: elevated; mortality begins to rise
• >4 mmol/L: severe; strongly predicts mortality, dictates aggressive resuscitation
• Lactate clearance ≥10% from baseline in 2 h is a quality measure equivalent to ScvO₂ ≥70%.
Inline MCQ 4. Initial lactate is 5.2 mmol/L. After 2 hours of resuscitation, repeat lactate is 4.7. The lactate clearance is:
A. ~25%, adequate
B. ~15%, adequate
C. ~10%, borderline; continue aggressive resuscitation
D. Lactate clearance is irrelevant in sepsis
Answer: C. Clearance = (5.2-4.7)/5.2 ≈ 10%. Borderline — target is ≥10–20% over 2 h. Reference: Tintinalli p.1001 + SSC guidelines.

5. Screening Tools — qSOFA & SOFA

Tintinalli p.999

qSOFA (quick SOFA) — bedside screening for adults outside ICU

CriterionCut-off
Respiratory rate≥22 breaths/min
Altered mental statusGCS <15
Systolic BP≤100 mm Hg

Score ≥2 — high risk of sepsis-related mortality; consider further evaluation. Note: qSOFA is a screening tool, not a definition — its sensitivity is limited (~60-70%), so a negative qSOFA does NOT rule out sepsis if clinical suspicion is high.

Full SOFA score (used in ICU and for Sepsis-3 confirmation)

Six organ systems each scored 0–4 points. Increase ≥2 points from baseline = sepsis (with infection).

  • Respiration: PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio
  • Coagulation: platelets
  • Liver: bilirubin
  • Cardiovascular: MAP and vasopressor requirement
  • CNS: GCS
  • Renal: creatinine and urine output

SIRS (still used for screening despite Sepsis-3)

≥2 of 4:

  • Temp >38°C or <36°C
  • HR >90/min
  • RR >20/min or PaCO₂ <32 mm Hg
  • WBC >12,000 or <4,000 or >10% bands
SIRS is sensitive but NOT specific — many non-infectious conditions (trauma, pancreatitis, burns) trigger SIRS criteria. Use clinical judgment.
Inline MCQ 5. Which is the qSOFA threshold to consider high mortality risk?
A. ≥1 of 3 criteria
B. ≥2 of 3 criteria
C. All 3 of 3 criteria
D. qSOFA score ≥4
Answer: B. qSOFA ≥2 of (RR ≥22, AMS, SBP ≤100) — high risk. Reference: Tintinalli p.999.

6. Treatment — Surviving Sepsis Bundle

Tintinalli p.1001

1-Hour Bundle (SSC 2018 update)

To be completed within 1 hour of recognition (was 3 h + 6 h previously):

  1. Measure lactate; remeasure if initial >2.
  2. Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics (do not delay if access difficult).
  3. Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 h.
  4. Begin rapid crystalloid 30 mL/kg for hypotension or lactate ≥4 mmol/L.
  5. Initiate vasopressors if BP not responsive to fluids — target MAP ≥65 mm Hg.
⚠️ Time matters: Each hour of delay in antibiotic administration in septic shock is associated with ~7% increase in mortality. Antibiotics ASAP after blood cultures (or sooner if cultures cannot be obtained quickly).

Beyond the 1-h bundle

  • Reassess hemodynamics, repeat lactate, ScvO₂ ≥70%.
  • Source control within 6–12 h of recognition.
  • Steroid therapy if vasopressor-dependent (next section).
  • Glycemic control: target glucose <180 mg/dL.
  • DVT prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis, nutrition support.

7. Fluid Resuscitation

Tintinalli p.1001
  • Initial bolus: 30 mL/kg balanced crystalloid (NS or LR/Plasmalyte) within 1 h.
  • Type: balanced crystalloid (LR/Plasmalyte) preferred over 0.9% NS in larger volumes — less risk of hyperchloremic acidosis. SMART/SALT-ED trials support balanced.
  • NOT recommended: hydroxyethyl starch (↑ AKI), albumin routinely (consider if cirrhosis, large volumes).
  • Reassess response after each bolus — do not chase numbers blindly.

Assessing fluid responsiveness

  • Static markers (CVP, IVC diameter alone) — poor.
  • Dynamic markers (preferred):
    • Passive leg raise (≥10% increase in CO/SV)
    • Stroke volume variation >13% (mechanically ventilated)
    • IVC collapsibility >50% in spontaneously breathing
    • End-expiratory occlusion test
Avoid over-resuscitation: excessive fluids → pulmonary edema, cerebral edema, abdominal compartment syndrome, ↑ mortality. After initial 30 mL/kg, base further fluids on dynamic responsiveness assessment.
Inline MCQ 6. Which is the recommended initial crystalloid volume for septic shock with hypotension?
A. 10 mL/kg over 30 min
B. 20 mL/kg only
C. 30 mL/kg balanced crystalloid within 3 h (1 h per latest)
D. 60 mL/kg over 6 h regardless
Answer: C. 30 mL/kg crystalloid within 1 h is the SSC standard. Balanced crystalloid preferred. Reference: Tintinalli + SSC 2018.

9. Vasopressors & Adjuncts

Tintinalli p.1001–1002

Vasopressors

  1. Norepinephrine — first-line. Start 0.05 µg/kg/min; titrate to MAP ≥65.
  2. Vasopressin — second agent (0.03 U/min, fixed dose, no titration); spares NE dose.
  3. Epinephrine — third agent or for septic cardiomyopathy with low CO.
  4. Dobutamine — added if persistent low CO despite adequate MAP and volume.
  5. Dopamine — fallen out of favor; reserved for select bradycardia cases (↑ arrhythmia risk).
  6. Phenylephrine — limited role; consider if NE-induced tachyarrhythmia.

Steroids

  • Hydrocortisone 200 mg/day IV (50 mg q6h or continuous infusion).
  • Indication: septic shock with vasopressor-dependent MAP despite adequate fluids.
  • Trials: ADRENAL (2018) showed faster shock reversal; mortality benefit modest.

Other adjuncts

  • Glucose control: target <180 mg/dL (avoid tight control <110 — increased mortality).
  • Transfusion: Hgb threshold 7 g/dL (target 7–9) unless ACS, active bleeding, profound shock.
  • Mechanical ventilation: low tidal volume 6 mL/kg PBW for ARDS.
  • Renal replacement therapy for AKI as needed; CRRT vs intermittent — no mortality difference.
Inline MCQ 8. First-line vasopressor for septic shock is:
A. Dopamine
B. Norepinephrine
C. Vasopressin alone
D. Phenylephrine
Answer: B. NE first-line; vasopressin second. Dopamine has fallen from favor due to arrhythmia risk. Reference: SSC 2021 + Tintinalli.

10. Source Control

Tintinalli p.1001

"Antibiotics alone are not enough" — the source of infection must be physically addressed when feasible.

  • Abscess drainage — bedside or interventional.
  • Necrotizing fasciitis / abdominal source — emergency surgery.
  • Foreign body / line removal if line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI).
  • Nephrostomy / drainage for obstructive pyelonephritis.
  • Cholecystectomy / biliary drainage for ascending cholangitis (Charcot's triad / Reynolds' pentad).
  • Goal: source control within 6–12 h of recognition.
Necrotizing soft-tissue infections: mortality nearly 100% without surgical debridement. Antibiotics + supportive care alone are inadequate. Recognize early signs: pain out of proportion, crepitus, bullae, "dishwater" drainage, rapid progression.

11. Disposition

Tintinalli p.1002
  • ICU admission: septic shock, vasopressor needs, mechanical ventilation, severe organ dysfunction.
  • Step-down/intermediate care: sepsis with stable vitals and modest organ dysfunction not requiring ICU level care.
  • General ward: sepsis without shock, after ED resuscitation; antibiotic regimen optimized; clear source control plan.
  • Discharge home: rare in true sepsis; consider only after extensive ED stabilization, reliable follow-up, and only mild forms of localized infection without systemic signs.
  • Transfer: consider tertiary care for source control beyond local capability (advanced surgery, ICU resources).

⚕️ ATLS 11th — Sepsis in Trauma

ATLS — Special Considerations
  • Trauma patients ≥48 h post-injury are at high risk for nosocomial pneumonia, line infections, and surgical-site infections leading to sepsis.
  • Differential of fever in trauma: infection, atelectasis, DVT/PE, drug fever, transfusion reaction.
  • Septic shock vs hemorrhagic shock can be difficult to distinguish in late trauma — focused volume status assessment + lactate trend + bedside US guide therapy.
  • Mass transfusion + sepsis: dilutional coagulopathy + DIC of sepsis — both need correction (FFP, cryo, platelets, balanced ratios).
  • Source control in trauma: line/wound/drain re-evaluation, abscess drainage, possible OR for missed injury.

📗 Rosen's EM 10th — Sepsis

Rosen's Ch.6 (Shock) + Ch.122 (Sepsis)
  • Same Sepsis-3 framework, with similar emphasis on lactate, qSOFA, time-to-antibiotic.
  • Rosen's notes passive leg raise is the most validated bedside dynamic responsiveness test in spontaneously breathing patients.
  • Rosen's emphasizes balanced crystalloid (LR/Plasmalyte) over 0.9% NS for sepsis resuscitation, citing SMART trial.
  • Detailed algorithm for septic cardiomyopathy management — bedside echo, dobutamine if low CO with adequate volume.
  • Rosen's stresses that steroids do not reduce mortality overall (large RCTs neutral) but reduce shock duration — give if vasopressor-dependent.
  • Procalcitonin trend can support antibiotic discontinuation in stable patients (not initial decision).

🔑 Key Points (High-Yield)

  1. Sepsis-3: infection + organ dysfunction (SOFA Δ ≥2). Septic shock = sepsis + vasopressor for MAP ≥65 + lactate >2 despite fluids.
  2. qSOFA bedside: RR ≥22, AMS, SBP ≤100. ≥2 of 3 → high mortality risk.
  3. 1-h bundle: lactate + cultures + antibiotics + 30 mL/kg crystalloid + vasopressors as needed.
  4. Antibiotics ASAP — each hour of delay → ~7% ↑ mortality.
  5. Norepinephrine first-line vasopressor; vasopressin second; epi/dobutamine for cardiomyopathy.
  6. Hydrocortisone 200 mg/day only if vasopressor-dependent shock.
  7. Lactate >4 = severe; clearance ≥10–20% in 2 h is the target.
  8. Balanced crystalloid (LR/Plasmalyte) preferred over 0.9% NS for large volumes.
  9. Source control within 6–12 h — abscess drainage, necrotizing fasciitis to OR, line removal for CLABSI.
  10. Cryptic shock in elderly: normal vitals + ↑ lactate. Don't miss it.
  11. Avoid: dopamine (arrhythmias), hydroxyethyl starch (AKI), tight glucose <110 (mortality), low TV mechanical ventilation if ARDS.
  12. Glucose target <180 mg/dL (not too tight); Hgb threshold 7 g/dL.

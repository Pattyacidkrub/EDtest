Gastrointestinal Disorders

Peptic Ulcer Disease and Gastritis

PUD, gastritis, and dyspepsia are common ED labels, but they are presumptive until dangerous mimics and complications are excluded. The ED task is to find bleeding, perforation, obstruction, malignancy alarm features, ACS/AAA/pancreatitis/biliary mimics, and NSAID/H. pylori risk.

Board trap: Antacid response does not prove benign ulcer disease; ACS and AAA can still present as epigastric pain.

PEPTIC ULCER DISEASE AND GASTRITIS

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

Peptic ulcers are recurrent ulcerations of the stomach or proximal duodenum. H. pylori and NSAIDs account for most disease, while gastritis refers to mucosal inflammation and dyspepsia is a symptom syndrome that may be structural or functional.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Acid and pepsin injure mucosa when protective mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandin support, and mucosal blood flow are overwhelmed. H. pylori disrupts the mucous layer and changes gastrin/mucus/bicarbonate balance; NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-mediated protection. Rosen highlights that stress, shock, ethanol, glucocorticoids, bile, and pancreatic secretions can also damage the gastric mucosal barrier.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Classic PUD produces burning epigastric pain, sometimes sharp/dull/hungry, relieved by food/milk/antacids and recurring when the stomach empties or at night. Older adults may have minimal symptoms or atypical nausea, anorexia, weight loss, or bleeding. Acute gastritis commonly presents with GI bleeding from occult blood loss to massive hemorrhage.

A change in the character of typical pain is a complication warning: abrupt generalized pain suggests perforation; rapid mid-back pain suggests posterior penetration into the pancreas; persistent vomiting suggests gastric outlet obstruction; hematemesis, melena, hematochezia, weakness, or syncope suggests bleeding.

DIAGNOSIS

Definitive PUD diagnosis requires endoscopy; the ED usually makes a risk-stratified presumptive diagnosis. The differential includes GERD, biliary disease, pancreatitis, hepatitis, AAA, gastroparesis, functional dyspepsia, malignancy, mesenteric ischemia, and myocardial ischemia.

ANCILLARY TESTING

Testing is directed by mimic/complication risk: CBC for anemia/bleeding, LFTs/lipase for hepatobiliary/pancreatic disease, ECG/troponin for cardiac mimics, acute abdominal series or CT for perforation/obstruction, bedside US for gallstones or AAA, and H. pylori testing when follow-up strategy supports it.

TREATMENT

Uncomplicated suspected PUD/gastritis is treated with acid suppression, antacids for breakthrough pain, stopping NSAIDs when possible, and follow-up for definitive evaluation. Known H. pylori positive disease needs eradication therapy; alarm features mandate early endoscopy/consultation.

PROTON PUMP INHIBITORS

PPIs irreversibly inhibit parietal cell H+/K+ ATPase and are preferred first-line noneradication therapy. Take 30-60 minutes before a meal. Long-term PPI risks and drug interactions matter, but in ED ulcer bleeding or high-risk ulcer disease, benefit often outweighs theoretical concerns.

H2 RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS

H2 blockers inhibit histamine-mediated acid secretion and are useful for mild disease or step-down therapy. Dose adjust in renal failure; cimetidine has more drug interactions than alternatives.

OTHER AGENTS

Sucralfate coats ulcerated surfaces but can constipate and bind other medications. Antacids can impair absorption of warfarin, digoxin, anticonvulsants, and antibiotics. Misoprostol may prevent NSAID ulcers but is abortifacient and not used in patients who could become pregnant without reliable contraception.

H. PYLORI ERADICATION

Eradication is indicated when H. pylori is diagnosed with ulcer disease. Resistance patterns drive regimen choice; Rosen shows typical triple and bismuth quadruple options as a practical reference.

COMPLICATIONS

BLEEDING

Hemorrhage is the most common serious PUD complication. Treat as upper GI bleeding: resuscitation, type/cross, transfusion when clinically indicated, PPI therapy, and early endoscopy for hemostasis. ICU-level care is indicated for significant bleeding or instability.

PERFORATION

Perforation causes abrupt severe epigastric pain with chemical then bacterial peritonitis. Elderly patients may not show dramatic peritoneal signs. If suspected: two large-bore IVs, oxygen if needed, monitor, CBC/type-cross/lipase, NG suction, broad-spectrum antibiotics, imaging, and prompt surgical consultation. Free air plus peritonitis is enough to act.

OBSTRUCTION

Gastric outlet obstruction causes fullness, nausea, vomiting, distention, succussion splash, dehydration, and electrolyte abnormalities. Treat with IV fluids, electrolyte correction, NG decompression, and admission; surgery or endoscopic intervention may be needed.

DISPOSITION AND FOLLOW-UP

Complications require consultation/admission. Uncomplicated suspected PUD/gastritis can often discharge after dangerous mimics are excluded, with acid suppression, NSAID/alcohol/tobacco avoidance, and follow-up. Alarm features, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, weight loss, dysphagia/odynophagia, anemia, mass, or unresolved critical differential diagnoses require early endoscopy or admission. Discharge instructions must say the diagnosis is presumptive and list return precautions.

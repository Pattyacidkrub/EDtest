Toxicology Chapter 182

Barbiturates

Barbiturate poisoning is a sedative-hypnotic coma problem where airway, ventilation, temperature, perfusion, and exposure duration decide outcome.

Board frame: there is no specific antidote; phenobarbital is the agent where multidose charcoal and dialysis discussions matter most.

Pharmacology and Exposure Sorting

Barbiturates enhance GABA-A inhibition. Compared with benzodiazepines, they can produce deeper CNS depression because they prolong chloride-channel opening and at high concentrations can directly activate the receptor. Toxicity is worse with ethanol, opioids, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, or other sedatives.

Duration matters. Ultrashort agents such as thiopental and methohexital are procedure drugs, while butalbital appears in headache products and phenobarbital remains important for seizures and withdrawal. Phenobarbital has a long half-life and partial renal elimination, so it is the classic enhanced-elimination barbiturate.

Clinical Features

Mild intoxication resembles ethanol intoxication with drowsiness, slurred speech, ataxia, nystagmus, and poor coordination. Severe poisoning causes coma, respiratory depression, hypothermia, hypotension, ileus, and loss of protective reflexes. Early death is usually respiratory arrest or cardiovascular collapse.

Pupils and nystagmus are variable, so they should not be used as a single diagnostic shortcut. Skin bullae or “coma blisters” can occur after prolonged pressure and hypoxia but are not specific to barbiturates.

Respiratory

Central hypoventilation, aspiration, pulmonary edema, acute lung injury.

Cardiovascular

Vasodilation and myocardial depression can produce shock, especially with hypoxia or coingestion.

Metabolic

Hypoglycemia and hypothermia can complicate prolonged coma.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical first. Obtain bedside glucose, chemistries, ECG, CBC when relevant, coingestant testing such as acetaminophen/salicylate/ethanol, and ABG/VBG or chest imaging when respiratory failure or aspiration is suspected. A urine barbiturate immunoassay may support exposure but does not prove toxic concentration.

False-positive urine barbiturate screens have been reported with ibuprofen and naproxen. Serum phenobarbital levels can document and trend toxicity, but levels do not replace serial neurologic and respiratory reassessment, especially in tolerant patients or mixed overdoses.

Do not anchor: persistent coma, focal neurologic deficits, trauma, papilledema, unexpected hyperthermia, clonus, or severe acidosis should trigger a search for other toxicologic and non-toxicologic causes.

Treatment

Airway and Stabilization

Airway and ventilation come first. Intubate when protective reflexes or ventilation are inadequate. Treat hypoglycemia, monitor core temperature, actively rewarm hypothermia, and support blood pressure with isotonic fluids followed by vasopressors when shock persists.

Gastrointestinal Decontamination

Single-dose activated charcoal may be considered early after a potentially life-threatening ingestion if the patient is cooperative or the airway is protected. Gastric lavage is generally not indicated. Whole-bowel irrigation is not routine for typical barbiturate ingestion.

Complications

Manage aspiration, pressure injury, rhabdomyolysis, hypothermia, and prolonged immobilization. Naloxone can be given when opioid coingestion is possible, but it does not reverse barbiturate toxicity.

Enhanced Elimination

Multidose activated charcoal can increase phenobarbital clearance in selected severe cases by binding drug during enterohepatic and enteroenteric circulation. Use it only when bowel function and airway safety are acceptable.

Urinary alkalinization may increase phenobarbital ionization and renal elimination, but evidence is limited and it is not first-line. Dialysis or hemoperfusion should be discussed for severe phenobarbital toxicity with persistent coma, refractory hypotension, renal or cardiac failure, or very high persistent serum levels despite optimal supportive care and MDAC.

Practical sequence: resuscitation first, poison center/toxicology early, then decide MDAC, alkalinization, or hemodialysis based on phenobarbital severity and trajectory.

Barbiturate Withdrawal

Dependence can develop with chronic use. Abrupt discontinuation may produce anxiety, tremor, hallucinations, autonomic instability, delirium, and seizures, similar to severe ethanol or benzodiazepine withdrawal. Because withdrawal can be fatal, high-risk patients need medically supervised gradual taper or substitution, not abrupt cessation.

Disposition

Asymptomatic patients after ingestion require observation until mental status, respiratory status, and vital signs remain stable and coingestants are addressed. Symptomatic patients need monitored care; coma, respiratory depression, shock, hypothermia, aspiration, or need for enhanced elimination generally requires ICU-level management.

After intentional ingestion, psychiatric evaluation is required once medically safe. Chronic users need a withdrawal plan before discharge.

Key Points

No antidote

Supportive care is the treatment spine.

Phenobarbital matters

Long acting, level-trendable, and sometimes removable.

Do not trust UDS alone

Qualitative screens do not prove causality or severity.

Withdrawal can kill

Dependent users need supervised tapering.

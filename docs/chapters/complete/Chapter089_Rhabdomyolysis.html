Renal and Genitourinary Disorders

Rhabdomyolysis

Rhabdomyolysis is muscle destruction with leakage of CK, myoglobin, potassium, phosphate, uric acid, and other intracellular contents. The ED job is to recognize the cause, prevent kidney injury, and catch hyperkalemia, compartment syndrome, DIC, and renal failure early.

Board trap: The classic triad is not required; normal-looking urine or a normal exam can still be rhabdomyolysis when the risk story fits.

RHABDOMYOLYSIS

INTRODUCTION AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

Adult causes include drugs of abuse and alcohol, medications, trauma/crush, seizures, immobilization, heat illness, exertion, infection, muscle disease, and inherited metabolic disorders. Multiple causes often coexist. In children, viral myositis and trauma are common and the course is usually more benign. Recurrent episodes after fasting or exercise suggest inherited metabolic myopathy.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

The final common pathway is ATP pump failure and calcium dysregulation. Na/K ATPase and calcium transport fail, intracellular calcium rises, phospholipase/proteases activate, mitochondrial dysfunction worsens, and myocytes die. Once muscle breaks down, the kidney is hit by volume depletion, aciduria, tubular obstruction, renal vasoconstriction, and pigment toxicity.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Symptoms include myalgias, weakness, malaise, low-grade fever, dark brown urine, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, tachycardia, mental status change from uremia, focal swelling/tenderness, and sometimes hemorrhagic skin changes. Postural muscles of thighs, calves, and lower back are common. Muscle swelling may become more apparent after IV fluids.

Absence of dark urine does not exclude rhabdomyolysis; myoglobin clears quickly and only a minority have documented myoglobinuria.

DIAGNOSIS

Serum CK is the most sensitive and reliable marker. A common diagnostic threshold is about five times the upper limit of normal, roughly 800 to 1000 IU/L when cardiac or brain injury is not the source. CK rises 2 to 12 hours after injury, peaks at 24 to 72 hours, and should fall about 39% per day once injury stops; failure to fall suggests ongoing muscle necrosis.

Myoglobin causes red-brown urine but urine dipstick cannot distinguish RBCs, hemoglobin, and myoglobin. Blood-positive dipstick with zero or rare RBCs on microscopy should trigger myoglobinuria/hemoglobinuria thinking. Check electrolytes, calcium, phosphorus, uric acid, BUN/creatinine, urinalysis, CBC/coagulation if severe, ECG when hyperkalemia is possible, and directed testing for the cause.

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Consider acute myopathies, periodic paralysis, polymyositis or dermatomyositis, Guillain-Barre syndrome, focal vascular/bony injury, compartment syndrome, infection, heat illness, toxicologic syndromes, and inherited metabolic myopathy when episodes recur with exercise or fasting.

TREATMENT

PREHOSPITAL CARE

For crush injury, strongly suspected rhabdomyolysis, prolonged extrication, or long transport, early vigorous IV fluids are the key kidney-protective intervention. Start normal saline during extrication when feasible; avoid potassium- or lactate-containing solutions until potassium and acid-base status are known.

EMERGENCY DEPARTMENT CARE

Continue aggressive IV crystalloid rehydration for 24 to 72 hours. One strategy is rapid deficit correction followed by about 4 mL/kg/h with urine output goal 3 to 4 mL/kg/h; another target is 200 to 300 mL/h. Place a urinary catheter in critical patients or those with AKI to measure output and use cardiac monitoring for electrolyte dysrhythmias.

No prospective controlled evidence supports routine bicarbonate, mannitol, or loop diuretics for every patient. If bicarbonate is used, keep it isotonic and avoid alkalosis or hypokalemia. Mannitol can worsen hypovolemia. Treat hyperkalemia aggressively; calcium is used for hyperkalemic cardiotoxicity or profound symptomatic hypocalcemia, not routine early hypocalcemia. Avoid NSAIDs.

DISEASE COMPLICATIONS

Complications include AKI, acidosis or alkalosis, hyperkalemia, calcium/phosphate abnormalities, DIC, compartment syndrome, and peripheral nerve injury. Rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI is promoted by hypovolemia, acidosis/aciduria, uric acid and myoglobin precipitation, tubular obstruction, renal vasoconstriction, and direct pigment toxicity.

CK level alone is not a reliable prognosis tool. Traumatic rhabdomyolysis has a stronger CK-AKI relationship, but risk tools such as McMahon score use creatinine, calcium, CK, phosphate, bicarbonate, etiology, sex, and age to estimate death or dialysis risk.

DISPOSITION AND FOLLOW-UP

Healthy patients with exertional rhabdomyolysis, no heat stress/dehydration/trauma, normal vital signs, normal renal function/electrolytes, good oral intake, improving symptoms, and reliable follow-up may be observed and discharged. Otherwise admit for IV hydration, monitoring, complication treatment, and etiology management. At least the first 24 to 48 hours often needs a monitored bed when dysrhythmias or worsening renal function are concerns. Consult nephrology for dialysis concern, refractory hyperkalemia, or high McMahon risk score.

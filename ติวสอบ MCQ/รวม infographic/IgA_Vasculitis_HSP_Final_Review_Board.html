IgA Vasculitis (HSP)

Small-vessel leukocytoclastic vasculitis due to IgA immune-complex deposition

1Recognize

Most common vasculitis of childhood. Tintinalli describes typical age as 3-15 years, peak 5-7 years; abdominal-pain chapter describes children 2-11 years.

  • Usually preceded by an upper respiratory tract infection.
  • Associated triggers reported: respiratory pathogens, medications, and vaccines.
  • Male and female children are affected equally in Tintinalli's rash chapter.
  • Self-limited in most children, but recurrence occurs in about one third.
Pathology

IgA, C3, and immune-complex deposition in small vessels involving skin, GI tract, kidneys, joints, and other tissues.

Histology classically shows a leukocytoclastic vasculitis.

Typical Exam Stem

Child with palpable nonblanching purpura on legs/buttocks, colicky abdominal pain, knee/ankle arthralgia, and hematuria/proteinuria.

3Dangerous Mimics
Think of other causes
  • Meningococcemia or sepsis
  • Idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura
  • Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura
  • Hemolytic-uremic syndrome
  • Intussusception
  • Pancreatitis
  • Septic arthritis or osteomyelitis
Clues against simple HSP
  • Toxic appearance or shock
  • High fever or meningismus
  • Abnormal platelet count
  • Focal unilateral hot joint
  • Severe persistent abdominal pain
  • Bilious vomiting or obstruction pattern
  • Renal failure, severe hypertension, or edema
2Classic Tetrad
dermatology
Palpable Purpura
  • Rash occurs in nearly all patients.
  • Nonblanching, palpable, nonpruritic purpura.
  • Predominantly buttocks, legs, and arms; lower extremities common.
  • Lesions often 2-10 mm.
gastroenterology
Abdominal Pain
  • Colicky abdominal pain occurs in about half; GI symptoms may occur in 50-75%.
  • Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, or GI bleeding may be the chief complaint.
  • Abdominal pain can precede rash in 30-43%.
  • Intussusception occurs in about 3.5%.
orthopedics
Arthralgia / Arthritis
  • Lower-extremity joints: knees and ankles most common.
  • Arthralgia/arthritis occurs in 70-85% or about 80% depending on chapter framing.
  • Usually migratory, periarticular swelling, nonerosive, self-limited.
  • Often bilateral; fever is usually absent.
nephrology
Renal Involvement
  • Renal disease may be asymptomatic.
  • Renal complications occur in 20-54%; rash chapter says approximately half.
  • Microscopic/gross hematuria, proteinuria, hypertension, edema, nephritic syndrome.
  • Persistent inflammation is why follow-up matters.
Remember: The tetrad may occur in any order, and not all patients have all four findings at presentation.
4Workup
Urinalysis with microscopy is mandatory. Look for RBCs, casts, and protein.
Blood pressure at diagnosis. Hypertension suggests renal involvement until proven otherwise.
Renal function tests if hematuria, proteinuria, hypertension, edema, dehydration, or ill appearance.
CBC / platelets / coagulation studies only when presentation is atypical or mimics are possible.
Stool occult blood if GI symptoms; positive guaiac can occur and alone does not require further workup.
Abdominal US for severe pain, bilious vomiting, obstruction signs, or intussusception concern.
ASO / strep testing may be considered if post-streptococcal trigger suspected.
Labs do not alter routine treatment when the bedside diagnosis is classic and renal screen is normal.
Renal risk factors described in Tintinalli: male sex, age >10 years, abdominal pain, GI bleeding, persistent purpura, elevated ASO, WBC >15,000/mm3, and platelets >500 x 109/L.
5Treatment & Doses

Supportive Care

  • Reassure: usually self-limited.
  • Hydration; encourage oral fluids if tolerated.
  • Rest and avoid strenuous activity while symptomatic.
  • Assess intake, output, vomiting, and dehydration.
Acetaminophen: 10-15 mg/kg/dose PO q4-6h PRN

Usual max: 75 mg/kg/day or adult max 4 g/day, whichever is lower.

Pain / Arthralgia

Ibuprofen: 10 mg/kg/dose PO q6-8h PRN

Usual max: 40 mg/kg/day.

Ketorolac: 0.5 mg/kg IV/IM q6h PRN

Use local pediatric max dose policy; avoid prolonged use.

Avoid NSAIDs if:
  • Renal involvement or rising creatinine
  • Dehydration or poor oral intake
  • GI bleeding or significant abdominal symptoms

Severe GI or Joint Symptoms

Prednisone: 1 mg/kg/day PO x 2 weeks, then taper over 2 weeks

Use for severe abdominal pain or significant joint symptoms after mimics and complications are considered.

Steroids can attenuate joint and GI symptoms.
They are not proven to prevent nephritis or long-term renal damage.

Severe nephritis: discuss with pediatric nephrology; therapies are controversial and specialist-led.

6Disposition & Follow-Up
Disposition Criteria Next Step
Outpatient Stable vitals, tolerating PO, pain controlled, no severe GI symptoms, normal BP, normal or mild renal screen. Repeat UA and BP within 7 days; continue surveillance for 6 months.
Admit / observe Ill appearing, dehydrated, unable to tolerate PO, uncontrolled pain, severe abdominal pain, GI bleeding, concern for intussusception. IV fluids, imaging as indicated, serial abdominal exams, pediatric/consult care.
Nephrology discussion Gross hematuria, more than small proteinuria, hypertension, edema, abnormal renal function, nephritic syndrome. Specialist-directed renal monitoring and therapy.

ecoPrognosis

Most children recover completely. Tintinalli notes HSP is typically self-limited and resolves over 3-4 weeks.

  • Recurrent disease: about one third.
  • Urinary abnormalities can persist for years.
  • Long-term renal impairment occurs in a small minority.
  • End-stage renal disease is rare.
schoolOne-Line Dx

IgA vasculitis / HSP = IgA immune-complex small-vessel vasculitis causing palpable purpura, abdominal pain, arthralgia/arthritis, and renal findings.
